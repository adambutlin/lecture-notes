## Equilibrium and the price mechanism {#s9}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Market equilibrium**: the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so no buyer or seller has reason to change their plans.\
> **Excess demand**: the shortfall of quantity supplied below quantity demanded at a price below equilibrium.\
> **Excess supply**: the surplus at a price above it.\
> **Functions of the price mechanism**: signalling where value is, incentivising producers to move resources there, and rationing what exists among competing buyers.

Equilibrium is the price at which plans are consistent: $Q_{D} = Q_{S}$. Using ({eq:e20}) and ({eq:e23}),

$$\alpha - \beta P + \gamma Y = \mu + \lambda P\quad \Rightarrow \quad P^{*} = \frac{\alpha - \mu + \gamma Y}{\beta + \lambda}\quad(\#e24)$$

The equilibrium price rises with anything that raises demand ($\alpha$, or $\gamma Y$ for a normal good), falls with anything that raises supply ($\mu$), and is divided by $\beta + \lambda$, the two sides' responsiveness to price added together. Substituting it back into either equation gives the quantity traded, and getting the same answer from both is a check on the algebra:

$$Q^{*} = \mu + \lambda P^{*} = \frac{\alpha\lambda + \beta\mu + \lambda\gamma Y}{\beta + \lambda}\quad(\#e25)$$

Unlike the diagram, the pair can be differentiated. Differentiating ({eq:e24}) with respect to income and with respect to the supply intercept:

$$\frac{dP^{*}}{dY} = \frac{\gamma}{\beta + \lambda},\quad\quad\frac{dP^{*}}{d\mu} = - \frac{1}{\beta + \lambda}\quad(\#e26)$$

A rise in income moves the price by the income sensitivity of demand divided by the combined responsiveness of both sides; an increase in supply lowers it by one pound per unit of $\mu$, divided by the same total. The common denominator is the result. Shocks on either side of the market move the price **more when** $\beta$ **and** $\lambda$ **are small**, that is, when demand and supply respond weakly to price. A good harvest raises supply in a market where demand for food barely responds to price, which is why it can crash food prices; a surge in demand for houses meets a supply of housing that responds slowly, which is why it mostly raises prices rather than the number of houses.

![Figure {fig:f11}. Equilibrium, and the adjustment to a rise in demand. At the old price $P_{0}$ there is excess demand.](figs/f11.png)

The algebra gives the destination and says nothing about the journey. Demand rises at every price, so at the old price $P_{0}$ the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied. Buyers compete for a limited quantity while sellers watch stocks run down, so sellers raise their prices. The higher price rations demand, because the buyers who valued the good least drop out first. It also rewards supply, because output that was not worth making at $P_{0}$ becomes worth making. Excess demand shrinks as both happen, and the process comes to rest at $P_{1}$, where $Q_{D} = Q_{S}$ once more.

The adjustment shows the three jobs prices do, which OCR calls the functions of the price mechanism: prices **signal** where value is, they **incentivise** producers to move resources there, and they **ration** what exists among competing buyers.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Every step of that adjustment is a claim about behaviour rather than a fact. It assumes sellers respond by raising prices rather than by rationing informally, that buyers drop out rather than queue, and, strongest of all, that the process settles rather than overshooting back and forth indefinitely.\
> Equilibrium is assumed to be a destination rather than a permanent condition. The model supplies a force pushing towards it and nothing more. Where prices are slow to move (wages, rents under contract) the disequilibrium can last, which is the seed of unemployment in Part III.
