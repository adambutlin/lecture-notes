## Supply {#s8}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Supply**: the quantity sellers are willing and able to sell at each price in a given period.\
> **Law of supply**: quantity supplied rises with price, other things equal.\
> **Firm's supply curve**: the part of its marginal cost curve above minimum average variable cost.

A price-taking firm sets $MR = MC$, and for a price taker $MR = P$, so it produces where $P = MC$. Read the other way round, its marginal cost curve tells us how much it will offer at each price: that is its supply curve. The qualification is section {sec:s6}'s shutdown rule. Below minimum average variable cost the firm produces nothing, so its supply curve is the part of $MC$ above minimum $AVC$. Section {sec:s3} explained why that part slopes upward: marginal cost is the wage divided by the marginal product, rising once diminishing returns set in.

Adding up firms gives market supply, written in linear form:

$$Q_{S} = \mu + \lambda P,\quad\quad\frac{\partial Q_{S}}{\partial P} = \lambda > 0\quad(\#e23)$$

$Q_{S}$ is the quantity supplied per period; $\lambda > 0$ is how many extra units firms offer for each £1 added to the price; and $\mu$ is the intercept, one number standing in for everything other than price that moves supply, principally input costs, technology, taxes on production, the number of firms and expectations.

$\mu$ is not the level of costs, and it moves the opposite way: cheaper inputs mean more is offered at any given price, so **lower** costs mean a **higher** $\mu$. Seen vertically, if each unit now costs £$h$ less to make, firms will accept £$h$ less for any given quantity, so the supply curve drops by exactly £$h$.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Many firms, each taking the price as given.\
> Input prices and technology fixed.\
> Firms maximising profit.\
> Drop the first assumption and there is no supply curve at all, as section {sec:s12} shows for monopoly.
