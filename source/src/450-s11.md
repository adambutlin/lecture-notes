## Welfare, surplus and efficiency {#s11}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Consumer surplus**: the difference between what buyers would have paid and what they did pay, summed over all units bought.\
> **Producer surplus**: the difference between the price received and marginal cost, summed the same way.\
> **Allocative efficiency**: output at the quantity where price equals marginal cost, so total surplus is at a maximum.\
> **Productive efficiency**: production at minimum average total cost.\
> **Welfare loss**: the surplus destroyed when output differs from the efficient quantity.

Section {sec:s7} established that, for a good taking a small share of the budget, the height of the demand curve is the willingness to pay for the last unit, in pounds. The demand curve therefore measures the **marginal private benefit** ($MPB$) of each unit, and the supply curve, as section {sec:s8} showed, the **marginal private cost** ($MPC$). That turns areas on the diagram into quantities of value: consumer surplus is the area between the demand curve and the price, and producer surplus is the area between the price and the supply curve.

![Figure {fig:f15}. Consumer and producer surplus at the competitive equilibrium.](figs/f15.png)

Why is $Q^{*}$ the best quantity rather than merely the equilibrium one? Define total surplus as the accumulated excess of benefit over cost:

$$TS(Q) = \int_{0}^{Q}\left\lbrack MPB(q) - MPC(q) \right\rbrack dq,\quad\quad\frac{dTS}{dQ} = MPB(Q) - MPC(Q)\quad(\#e30)$$

$TS(Q)$ is the total value the market has created by the time $Q$ units have been made. For each unit $q$ along the way, the integral subtracts what it cost to make from what it was worth to whoever bought it, and adds those gaps up from the first unit to the last. Differentiating undoes the adding up, so the value added by the very next unit is the gap between its benefit and its cost.

Setting the derivative to zero gives $MPB = MPC$, which is the condition $D = S$. Below $Q^{*}$ the next unit is worth more to somebody than it costs to make, so not making it wastes value. Above $Q^{*}$ the resources used are worth more elsewhere. The competitive equilibrium therefore achieves allocative efficiency, which OCR states as $P = MC$: price measures what the marginal buyer values the unit at, and marginal cost measures what society gives up to make it. Productive efficiency is a separate condition, and section {sec:s12} shows which market structures deliver it.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Surplus is measured by adding up willingness to pay, which assumes that ability to pay is an acceptable yardstick for value. A pound of surplus gained by a millionaire counts exactly as much here as a pound gained by someone on benefits.\
> Willingness to pay is measured in pounds only because the good is a small share of buyers' budgets (section {sec:s7}). For the household of section {sec:s1}, which spends half its budget on $x$ and buys some at any price, the area under demand is infinite and has no meaning as a sum of value.\
> Efficiency is a statement about the size of the pie and says nothing about its distribution, and it holds only when private benefit equals social benefit and private cost equals social cost. Section {sec:s13} removes that assumption and the conclusion collapses.
