## Money and banking {#s21}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Money**: anything generally accepted in payment for goods and services or in settlement of a debt.\
> **Barter**: direct exchange of goods for goods, with no money involved.\
> **Double coincidence of wants**: the requirement under barter that each party holds what the other wants, at the same moment.\
> **Liquidity**: how easily an asset can be turned into a means of payment without losing value.\
> **Narrow money**: notes, coin and commercial banks' reserves at the central bank.\
> **Broad money**: narrow money plus bank deposits, which is most of what people actually spend.\
> **Fractional reserve banking**: keeping only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lending the rest out.\
> **Reserve ratio**: the fraction of deposits a bank holds back as reserves.\
> **Money multiplier**: the deposits each pound of reserves can support, equal to one divided by the reserve ratio.

A baker has bread today. A shepherd will have wool in the spring after shearing, and wants bread now. Barter cannot help them, because barter needs a double coincidence of wants and needs it at a single moment. Handing the bread over now against a promise of wool in the spring solves it, but creates something new in the process: a debt.

Suppose instead that the baker wants leather, not wool. A tanner has leather and wants bread. A chain can still be built, bread to the tanner, leather to the baker, wool to whoever wants it in the spring, but every extra person, good and harvest date makes the bookkeeping harder. With a hundred people and a dozen goods, what limits trade is no longer the goods. It is the record of who owes what to whom.

The way out is to stop recording debts in particular goods at all. Hand something of value into the system and receive a standardised claim that can be passed on; hand the claim back later and receive something of value. The claim is worth holding because everybody expects everybody else to go on accepting it. That is money. A banknote has no use of its own. It is a record of a claim on real output that has been made transferable, and its four jobs follow from that:

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Function                       What it does
  ------------------------------ -----------------------------------------------------------
  Medium of exchange             removes the need for a double coincidence of wants

  Unit of account                puts every price on one scale, so any two can be compared

  Store of value                 separates the decision to sell from the decision to buy

  Standard of deferred payment   lets debts be written in fixed and enforceable terms
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Not everything can do those jobs, which is why money has tended to converge on a short list of properties:

  --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Property                      Why money needs it
  ----------------------------- --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Durable                       it has to survive being held and passed along

  Portable                      high value in a small weight, or carrying it costs more than it is worth

  Divisible                     it has to pay for small things as well as large ones

  Uniform                       one unit must be interchangeable with any other

  Limited in supply             if anybody can make more, nobody will hold it

  Generally accepted            the property that ultimately matters, and it rests on everyone else accepting it too
  --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Money and banking are separate inventions, and the second happened largely by accident. The usual account, and a fair description of seventeenth-century London, runs in three steps.

**Step one.** Merchants hold gold, which is heavy to carry and risky to keep. Goldsmiths already have strongrooms, so merchants deposit their gold and take away a paper receipt promising the bearer so much gold on demand.

**Step two.** Settling a debt by handing over the receipt is easier than fetching the gold, and the person receiving it is just as happy to hold the receipt. Receipts begin circulating instead of being redeemed. A private promise has become a means of payment.

**Step three.** The goldsmith notices that the gold sits almost entirely still. On any normal day only a small fraction of it is called for. So rather than leave it idle he issues new receipts to borrowers, who pay him interest for them. There are now more receipts in circulation than there is gold in the vault:

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Assets               £              Liabilities               £
  -------------------- -------------- ------------------------- --------------
  Gold in the vault    1,000          Receipts in circulation   4,000

  Loans to borrowers   3,000                                    

  **Total**            **4,000**      **Total**                 **4,000**
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The reserve ratio is 1,000 out of 4,000, or 25 per cent, and the receipts in circulation, which are the money supply, are four times the gold standing behind them. Nothing has been counterfeited and nobody has been defrauded. The depositor holds a claim he believes he can exercise whenever he likes, the borrower has purchasing power he did not have before, and the goldsmith earns interest on both. All three are satisfied at once, and they stay satisfied for as long as the depositors do not all arrive on the same morning.

Run the same logic through a system of banks rather than one goldsmith. Somebody deposits £1,000 of new reserves. The bank keeps a fraction $\rho$ of it and lends the rest. Whatever it lends is spent, and whoever receives the money banks it, so a second deposit of $(1 - \rho) \times$ £1,000 appears somewhere else in the system. That bank keeps $\rho$ of the new deposit and lends the rest, and the process repeats:

$$D = D_{0} + D_{0}(1 - \rho) + D_{0}(1 - \rho)^{2} + \cdots = D_{0}\sum_{n = 0}^{\infty}(1 - \rho)^{n}\quad \Rightarrow \quad D = \frac{D_{0}}{\rho}\quad(\#e48)$$

$D_{0}$ is the deposit that starts the process and $\rho$ the reserve ratio, so $(1 - \rho)$ is the fraction handed on at each round. Because $(1 - \rho)$ lies between zero and one the rounds shrink geometrically and the series sums to $1/\rho$. It is the same geometric series as the spending multiplier of section {sec:s17}, run through banks instead of through incomes.

Small reserve ratios imply very large multipliers:

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Reserve ratio $\rho$   Multiplier $1/\rho$   Deposits from £1,000 of reserves   New money created
  ---------------------- --------------------- ---------------------------------- -------------------
  50 per cent            2                     £2,000                             £1,000

  25 per cent            4                     £4,000                             £3,000

  10 per cent            10                    £10,000                            £9,000

  5 per cent             20                    £20,000                            £19,000

  2 per cent             50                    £50,000                            £49,000
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Halving the fraction banks hold back doubles the deposits a given quantity of reserves can support, so small changes in how cautious banks are have large effects on how much money exists. At a 2 per cent ratio, forty-nine of every fifty pounds in the economy were created by somebody taking out a loan.

Notes and coin come to roughly 3 per cent of the money supply in a developed economy, and the share has been drifting down for years as card payments have displaced cash. The two ratios are not quite the same object, since $\rho$ is a fraction banks choose to hold back while 3 per cent is what currency has ended up as a share of the total, but they point at the same fact: of the money you can actually spend, something like 97 pence in every pound is a bank deposit.

The story so far runs from reserves to deposits: the central bank supplies reserves and banks lend a multiple of them. Modern banking reverses the causation. Banks are not held back by a shortage of reserves; they create deposits by lending, when they find a borrower they judge creditworthy at a profitable rate, and the Bank of England supplies whatever reserves the system then needs at Bank Rate. The multiplier is a ceiling that rarely binds rather than a mechanism that drives anything. It is on the specification, so learn it, and learn its status. The money that section {sec:s22} puts on the horizontal axis of the money market is therefore mostly bank deposits created by lending, not notes issued by the state.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Every pound lent is assumed to come back to the banking system as somebody else's deposit. In practice some is held as cash and leaves the process, which lowers the multiplier.\
> Banks are assumed to lend out everything above the reserve ratio, and borrowers are assumed to want the loans. Neither holds in a downturn, when banks keep buffers well above the minimum and firms do not want to borrow at any rate.\
> The reserve ratio is assumed fixed.
