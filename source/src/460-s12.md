## Perfect competition and monopoly {#s12}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Perfect competition**: many firms, an identical product, free entry and perfect information, so every firm is a price taker.\
> **Monopoly**: a single seller protected by barriers to entry.\
> **Barrier to entry**: anything that stops new firms competing supernormal profit away.\
> **Mark-up**: the share of the price not accounted for by marginal cost, $(P - MC)/P$.\
> **Price discrimination**: charging different prices to different buyers for the same good, for reasons other than differences in cost.\
> **Dynamic efficiency**: improvement in products and processes over time.

Sections {sec:s2} to {sec:s6} apply to any firm. Industries differ in the demand curve each firm faces, which is set by the number of rivals, the height of barriers to entry, how similar the products are, and how well informed buyers are. This section takes the two extremes.

In perfect competition no firm can move the price, so it faces a horizontal demand curve and, by ({eq:e16}), $MR = AR = P$. Profit maximisation $MR = MC$ therefore becomes $P = MC$. If the price is above average total cost the firm makes supernormal profit, but free entry means new firms arrive, market supply moves down, and the price falls. Entry stops only when the profit that attracted it has gone:

$$P = MC = \min ATC\quad(\#e31)$$

Price has been driven down to the bottom of the average cost curve, and the firm just covers its costs, including the normal profit buried inside them. $P = MC$ is allocative efficiency, and producing at $\min ATC$ is productive efficiency. That is the benchmark against which every other structure is judged, which is the reason for studying a model whose assumptions are never all satisfied.

![Figure {fig:f16}. A competitive firm in the short run and after entry has driven price down to minimum average cost.](figs/f16.png)

The barriers that protect a monopoly can be legal (patents, licences), structural (a natural monopoly as in section {sec:s4}), or strategic. Because the firm is the industry, it faces the **whole market demand curve**, so $MR$ lies below $AR$. The firm of section {sec:s6} is such a firm. It sets $MR = MC$ at $Q_{m} = 87.3$ by ({eq:e32}) and reads its price off the demand curve, not off $MR$: $P_{m} = £8.76$, while marginal cost at that output is £3.53. Units worth more to buyers than they cost to produce are not made.

How much value is lost depends on what the alternative would have been. If the same marginal cost curve were instead the supply curve of many price-taking firms, output would rise to where demand meets it, $Q_{c} = 120.4$ at $P_{c} = £6.78$, and the triangle between demand and marginal cost from 87.3 to 120.4 units, about £93, is the welfare loss in Figure {fig:f17}. The comparison assumes a competitive industry could produce at the monopolist's costs, which is exactly what economies of scale may rule out.

The general statement comes from setting $MR = MC$ and substituting the elasticity form of marginal revenue from ({eq:e16}):

$$P\left( 1 + \frac{1}{PED} \right) = MC\quad \Rightarrow \quad\frac{P - MC}{P} = - \frac{1}{PED}\quad(\#e33)$$

The mark-up equals one over the elasticity of demand, with a minus sign because that elasticity is negative. In the example, demand at the chosen point has $PED = -1.67$, so the mark-up is $1/1.67 = 0.60$, and indeed $(8.76 - 3.53)/8.76 = 0.60$. A monopolist facing highly elastic demand, because of good substitutes or the threat of entry, prices close to marginal cost despite having no rivals today.

Two limits confirm that ({eq:e33}) behaves as it should. As demand becomes perfectly elastic, $PED \rightarrow - \infty$, the mark-up goes to zero and $P \rightarrow MC$, which is the competitive result. And since the mark-up cannot exceed one, $|PED|$ must be at least one at the chosen output: no price setter operates on the inelastic part of its demand curve.

![Figure {fig:f17}. Monopoly, drawn for the firm of section {sec:s6}. Output is set where $MR = MC$ and price is read off the demand curve above it. $Q_{c}$ and $P_{c}$ mark the outcome if the same marginal cost curve were a competitive industry's supply.](figs/f17.png)

A monopolist has no supply curve: its chosen quantity depends on the slope of demand as well as on marginal cost, so there is no single relationship between price and quantity supplied. Nor is monopoly simply worse. A protected profit stream can fund research, and economies of scale may make one large firm genuinely cheaper than many small ones.

Price discrimination drops the assumption of section {sec:s5} that every buyer pays the same price. It requires market power, buyer groups that can be told apart and that differ in elasticity, and no resale between them. With two groups, the firm chooses a quantity for each, and profit is

$$\Pi = P_{1}(Q_{1})Q_{1} + P_{2}(Q_{2})Q_{2} - TC(Q_{1} + Q_{2})\quad \Rightarrow \quad MR_{1} = MC = MR_{2}\quad(\#epd)$$

Differentiating with respect to each quantity in turn gives one first-order condition per market: the last unit sold in each must add the same to revenue as it adds to cost. If $MR_{1}$ were higher than $MR_{2}$, moving a unit of sales from market 2 to market 1 would raise revenue at no extra cost. Applying ({eq:e33}) market by market, each price carries the mark-up its own elasticity allows, so **the less elastic group pays more**.

Take marginal cost constant at £4, a student market with demand $P_{1} = 12 - Q_{1}$ and an adult market with $P_{2} = 20 - 2Q_{2}$. Setting $MR_{1} = 12 - 2Q_{1} = 4$ and $MR_{2} = 20 - 4Q_{2} = 4$ gives four units in each market, sold to students at £8 and to adults at £12. At those points $PED_{1} = -2$ and $PED_{2} = -1.5$, and the mark-ups are $4/8 = 1/2$ and $8/12 = 1/1.5$, as ({eq:e33}) requires. Charging one price instead, the firm would face the combined demand, sell the same 8 units at £9.33, and earn £42.67 rather than £48. With straight-line demands and both groups served, discrimination leaves total output unchanged and works purely by moving surplus from buyers to the firm. It raises output only when a single price would leave one group unserved altogether, which is the case usually made for it in markets such as medicines priced differently in rich and poor countries.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Both structures use the same profit-maximising firm of section {sec:s6} and differ only in the demand curve that firm faces, so each conclusion is only as good as that shared assumption.\
> Perfect competition assumes identical products, free entry and perfect information, none of which holds anywhere; its value is as the benchmark the others are measured against.\
> The welfare loss from monopoly is measured against a competitive industry with the monopolist's costs. Where economies of scale are large, that industry could not exist.
