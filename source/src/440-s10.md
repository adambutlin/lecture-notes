## Elasticity {#s10}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Elastic** means $|PED| > 1$, **inelastic** means $|PED| < 1$, and **unit elastic** means $|PED| = 1$, which is where total revenue peaks.\
> **Income elasticity (YED)**, **cross elasticity (XED)** and **price elasticity of supply (PES)** are the same construction as PED with income, another good's price, or the supply curve in place of the original terms.

Equation ({eq:e26}) said that shocks move price further when $\beta$ and $\lambda$ are small. Price elasticity of demand puts that sensitivity in proportional terms:

$$PED = \frac{dQ_{D}}{dP} \cdot \frac{P}{Q_{D}} = - \,\frac{\beta P}{Q_{D}}\quad(\#e27)$$

The gradient on its own would depend on whether petrol is measured in litres or in barrels; multiplying by $P/Q_{D}$ converts it into one percentage divided by another, and percentages have no units. Substituting $\partial Q_{D}/\partial P = - \beta$ from ({eq:e21}) gives the version on the right.

Elasticity is therefore a property of a **point**, not of a whole curve. On a straight line $\beta$ is constant but $P/Q_{D}$ is not: near the vertical intercept $Q_{D}$ is small and $|PED|$ is large, near the horizontal intercept $P$ is small and $|PED|$ tends to zero, and somewhere between them $|PED| = 1$. Gradient and elasticity are different objects, so "steeper means more inelastic" is only a rough guide and breaks down whenever two curves are compared at different quantities.

Raising the price does two opposing things to total revenue, $TR = PQ_{D}$, and the product rule separates them:

$$\frac{dTR}{dP} = Q_{D} + P\frac{dQ_{D}}{dP} = Q_{D}(1 + PED)\quad(\#e28)$$

The first term is the gain from charging more on every unit still sold; the second is the loss from selling fewer units. Since $PED < 0$, the bracket is positive when $|PED| < 1$ and negative when $|PED| > 1$. Raising price raises revenue on inelastic demand and destroys it on elastic demand, and revenue peaks where $|PED| = 1$, the same fact that appeared in section {sec:s5} as $MR = 0$.

The two terms are two areas on the diagram. Raise the price from $P_{0}$ to $P_{1}$ and quantity falls from $Q_{0}$ to $Q_{1}$. The seller **gains** the extra price on every unit still sold, a rectangle of height $P_{1} - P_{0}$ and width $Q_{1}$. The seller **loses** the old price on every unit no longer sold, a rectangle of width $Q_{0} - Q_{1}$ and height $P_{0}$. Revenue rises if the first rectangle is the larger.

![Figure {fig:f12}. The two rectangles behind equation ({eq:e28}). One price rise of 2 is applied at two points on the same demand curve: the gain is the shaded band above the old price, the loss is the block of units no longer sold.](figs/f12.png)

Both panels use the same demand curve, so only the point on the curve differs, and with it the ratio $P/Q_{D}$ in ({eq:e27}). High on the curve there are few units to gain on and many to lose, so the loss wins. Low on the curve there are many units to gain on and few to lose, so the gain wins.

![Figure {fig:f13}. Elasticity varies along a straight-line demand curve, and total revenue peaks where $|PED| = 1$.](figs/f13.png)

The same construction applied to other variables gives the rest of the family:

$$YED = \frac{\partial Q_{D}}{\partial Y} \cdot \frac{Y}{Q_{D}} = \frac{\gamma Y}{Q_{D}},\quad\quad XED = \frac{\partial Q_{D}}{\partial P_{j}} \cdot \frac{P_{j}}{Q_{D}},\quad\quad PES = \frac{dQ_{S}}{dP} \cdot \frac{P}{Q_{S}} = \frac{\lambda P}{Q_{S}}\quad(\#e29)$$

$P_{j}$ is the price of some other good $j$, so cross elasticity measures how demand for our good responds to it.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Elasticity     Response of         To a change in                  Reading the number
  -------------- ------------------- ------------------------------- --------------------------------------------------------
  $PED$          quantity demanded   the good's own price            below $- 1$ elastic, between $- 1$ and $0$ inelastic

  $YED$          quantity demanded   consumer income                 above $0$ normal, above $1$ luxury, below $0$ inferior

  $XED$          quantity demanded   the price of another good $j$   above $0$ substitutes, below $0$ complements

  $PES$          quantity supplied   the good's own price            larger with spare capacity and with time
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Demand is more elastic when close substitutes exist, when the good takes a large share of the budget, and when buyers have had time to adjust. Supply is more elastic when firms hold spare capacity, when inputs can be bought quickly, and again when there is time, which is the short-run and long-run distinction of sections {sec:s3} and {sec:s4} seen from the market's side.

Petrol has no close substitute for most journeys in the short run, so $\beta$ is small and $|PED|$ is well below one. Figure {fig:f14} shows what that implies when supply moves: with inelastic demand, a supply shift produces a large price change and a small quantity change, so almost the whole adjustment happens in price. With elastic demand the reverse holds.

![Figure {fig:f14}. The same upward shift in supply, a rise of 12 in unit costs, under inelastic and elastic demand. Elasticity decides how a shock splits between price and quantity.](figs/f14.png)

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Elasticities are local. Quoting a single PED for a good is really quoting an average over the range of prices actually observed, and extrapolating it far outside that range is not legitimate.
