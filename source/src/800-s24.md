## Supply-side policy and growth {#s24}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Supply-side policy**: measures intended to raise the productive capacity of the economy, moving long-run aggregate supply out.\
> **Market-based measures** work by sharpening incentives and removing obstacles; **interventionist measures** work by providing what markets under-provide.\
> **Total factor productivity (TFP)**: the part of output growth not explained by more labour or more capital, which captures technology and the efficiency with which inputs are combined.\
> **Hysteresis**: a lasting effect of a temporary shock, such as a recession that permanently lowers potential output.

Everything in sections {sec:s17}, {sec:s22} and {sec:s23} moves aggregate demand and therefore moves output only while money wages are sticky. Supply-side policy aims at $LRAS$ itself. An outward shift of $LRAS$ raises sustainable output and, for a given AD curve, lowers the price level, so it improves both targets at once. Because $LRAS$ is vertical, it can only move sideways, and a move to the right means more capacity.

![Figure {fig:lras}. An outward shift of long-run aggregate supply. Sustainable output rises from $Y_{FE}$ to $Y'_{FE}$ and, with aggregate demand unchanged, the price level falls.](figs/lras.png)

Market-based measures include cutting marginal income tax rates and corporation tax to raise the return to work and investment, deregulating product markets to sharpen competition, reducing trade barriers, privatisation, and reforms to labour law or benefit tapers intended to raise participation. Interventionist measures, which are section {sec:s13} applied at national scale, include education and training, infrastructure, subsidies to research and development, and support for regional development.

Growth can be split into its sources. Suppose output is produced from capital and labour by $Y = A K^{1/3} L^{2/3}$, where $A$ is total factor productivity. Taking logarithms gives $\ln Y = \ln A + \tfrac{1}{3}\ln K + \tfrac{2}{3}\ln L$, and differentiating with respect to time turns each logarithm into a growth rate:

$$g_{Y} = g_{A} + \tfrac{1}{3}\, g_{K} + \tfrac{2}{3}\, g_{L}\quad(\#egrowth)$$

$g_{Y}$, $g_{A}$, $g_{K}$ and $g_{L}$ are the growth rates of output, productivity, capital and labour. The weights are the exponents, and in a competitive economy they equal the shares of income paid to capital and labour, roughly a third and two thirds in the UK. If output grows by 2 per cent while capital grows by 3 per cent and labour by 1 per cent, capital accounts for 1 percentage point, labour for 0.67, and the remaining 0.33 points is TFP growth, measured as what is left over. Subtracting $g_{L}$ from both sides gives growth in output per worker, $g_{Y} - g_{L} = g_{A} + \tfrac{1}{3}(g_{K} - g_{L})$: it comes either from productivity or from more capital per worker. Capital per worker runs into the diminishing returns of section {sec:s2}, because each extra machine adds less when workers already have plenty, so only TFP growth can raise output per head indefinitely.

The choice in section {sec:s15} between consumer goods and capital goods was the choice between output now and capacity later. Investment, education and research are that choice made at national scale: they cost consumption today and shift the frontier outward for the following decade. Supply-side measures act slowly, often cost money up front, and many of them, especially the market-based ones, raise measured inequality while raising output. A policy can be correct about efficiency and still require a separate answer on distribution.

The separation between demand and supply is not complete. A deep or long recession can lower potential output itself: workers who stay unemployed for long periods lose skills and attachment to the labour market, firms cancel investment that would have added to the capital stock, and research is cut. This is hysteresis, and it means demand policy that shortens a recession can protect $LRAS$ as well as closing the output gap. The UK's productivity has grown much more slowly since the 2008 financial crisis than before it, and how much of that slowdown is hysteresis is still disputed.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Supply-side policy assumes the binding constraint is capacity rather than demand, so in a recession raising potential output does little for actual output.\
> Growth accounting assumes competitive factor markets, so that factor shares measure the contributions of capital and labour; TFP is then whatever is left over, including measurement error.\
> Market-based measures assume incentives are what hold output back; interventionist measures assume the government can identify and deliver what markets under-provide. Both work slowly, so their effects are hard to separate from everything else that happens over the same decade.
