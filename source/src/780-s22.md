## Monetary policy {#s22}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Monetary policy**: the use of interest rates and the quantity of money to meet the inflation target.\
> **Bank Rate**: the interest rate the Bank of England pays on commercial banks' reserves, which it sets and which is its main instrument.\
> **Real interest rate**: the nominal interest rate minus expected inflation, which measures the true cost of borrowing.\
> **Transmission mechanism**: the chain running from Bank Rate through market rates and spending to output and inflation.\
> **Quantitative easing**: central bank purchases of assets, used to lower longer-term yields once Bank Rate is close to zero.

The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee is set a symmetric CPI inflation target of 2 per cent, with a letter to the Chancellor required if inflation misses by more than one percentage point either way. Symmetric means undershooting is a failure too. Its instrument is Bank Rate, the rate it pays on the reserves commercial banks hold with it.

Bank Rate is not discovered by a market. The Bank announces it, and it holds because no bank will lend reserves to another for less than the Bank itself pays on them, while the Bank stands ready to lend reserves at that rate to any bank that needs them. Market rates on loans, mortgages and savings are set as margins over it. What the market then decides is the quantity of money. Households and firms choose how much of their wealth to hold as money rather than as interest-bearing assets:

$$M_{D} = \theta Y - \phi i\quad(\#e49)$$

$M_{D}$ is the quantity of money demanded, $Y$ is national income, and $i$ is the nominal interest rate. Demand for money rises with income, because more transactions have to be financed, by $\theta > 0$ for each extra £1; it falls with the interest rate, because interest forgone is the price of holding money, by $\phi > 0$ for each percentage point. With the Bank setting $i$ at Bank Rate, the quantity of money adjusts to whatever is demanded at that rate:

$$M = \theta Y - \phi\, i_{B},\quad\quad\frac{dM}{di_{B}} = - \phi\quad(\#e50)$$

Each percentage point on Bank Rate $i_{B}$ reduces the quantity of money people want to hold, and the bank lending that creates it, by $\phi$. The supply of money is horizontal at the rate the Bank has chosen, as section {sec:s21} anticipated: banks create deposits by lending, and the Bank supplies the reserves to support them at Bank Rate.

Exam questions usually draw the money supply as a vertical line instead, as if the Bank fixed a quantity of money and let the rate settle where that quantity is willingly held. The two diagrams describe the same equilibrium, a point on the money demand curve; they differ only in which variable the Bank chooses. To get a lower interest rate in the vertical-supply picture, the Bank must supply more money. In the horizontal one it lowers the rate and the quantity follows. The second is how the Bank of England operates, and the first is a convention worth being able to draw.

What matters for spending is the real interest rate, the nominal rate less the inflation borrowers expect:

$$r = i - \pi^{e}\quad(\#efisher)$$

A mortgage at 5 per cent when prices are expected to rise by 3 per cent costs 2 per cent a year in real terms. Because expectations move slowly, a change in Bank Rate changes real rates almost one for one in the short run, which is what gives monetary policy its grip.

The policy works through a chain of links. Bank Rate rises → banks' own funding costs rise, so mortgage, loan and deposit rates follow → borrowing becomes dearer and saving more attractive, so households cut spending on durables and housing and firms postpone investment projects whose expected return no longer clears the higher hurdle → $AD$ moves down → output falls and inflationary pressure eases.

Two further channels run alongside it. Higher rates make sterling assets more attractive, so capital flows in, the pound appreciates, exports lose competitiveness, and import prices fall, which reduces inflation directly as well as through demand. And the announcement itself moves expectations: if firms and unions believe the Bank will act, they moderate price and wage setting before output has moved at all, which is why credibility is treated as an instrument in its own right.

![Figure {fig:f31}. A demand expansion from full employment. Output rises from A to B in the short run, but once money wages adjust the economy settles at C, with a higher price level and output back at capacity.](figs/f31.png)

Starting at full employment at A, a demand expansion moves the economy up the short-run supply curve to B, where output is above capacity. Tight labour markets then raise money wages, $SRAS$ moves up, and the economy arrives at C: the same output as at A and a higher price level. In the long run demand policy determines the price level and not real output. Only the policies of section {sec:s24} move $Y_{FE}$.

Monetary policy works with long and variable lags, conventionally around eighteen months to two years in the UK, so the MPC must set rates against a forecast rather than against today's data. When Bank Rate is already near zero it cannot usefully be cut further, which is why quantitative easing was used after 2009: the Bank created reserves to buy government bonds, raising their prices and so lowering long-term interest rates, which Bank Rate influences only indirectly. Because the Bank pays Bank Rate on all reserves, the extra reserves QE created did not push short-term rates below the level the Bank had set. Its distributional effect is awkward: it works partly by raising the price of assets, which are held disproportionately by the already wealthy.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Each link of the transmission chain is an assumption rather than a fact. The first assumes pass-through: that market rates follow Bank Rate substantially and quickly. The second assumes consumption and investment respond to real interest rates, which is truer of housing than of most other spending. The third assumes the upward-sloping $SRAS$ of section {sec:s18}, so that lower demand shows up partly in prices and not only in lost output.\
> The exchange-rate and expectations channels add two more: that capital moves freely across borders, and that the Bank's announcements are believed.
