## Aggregate demand and the multiplier {#s17}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Aggregate demand**: total planned expenditure on domestic output at each price level.\
> **Marginal propensity to consume**: the fraction of each extra pound of disposable income that is spent.\
> **Marginal propensities to save, to tax and to import (MPS, MPT, MPM)**: the fractions of each extra pound of national income that are saved, taken in tax and spent on imports; their sum is the **marginal propensity to withdraw (MPW)**.\
> **Autonomous expenditure**: the part of planned spending that does not depend on income.\
> **Multiplier**: the number of pounds national income rises by for each extra pound of autonomous expenditure.\
> **Accelerator**: the relationship between investment and the change in output.

The simplest behavioural assumptions to add to the identity are:

$$C = C_{0} + cY_{d},\quad Y_{d} = (1 - \tau)Y,\quad M = mY,\quad I,\ G,\ X\ \text{taken as given}\quad(\#e43)$$

Households spend a fixed fraction $c$ of their take-home income plus an amount $C_{0}$ they would spend regardless; $c$ is the marginal propensity to consume, with $0 < c < 1$. Take-home income $Y_{d}$ is national income less the share $\tau$ (the Greek letter tau) taken in tax, a rate between zero and one. Spending on imports is a fixed fraction $m$ of income, the marginal propensity to import. Investment, government spending and exports are treated as set outside the model, which is a strong assumption.

Substituting these into the identity ({eq:e41}), writing $A = C_{0} + I + G + X$ for the part of expenditure that does not depend on income, and collecting the terms in $Y$:

$$Y = A + \left\lbrack c(1 - \tau) - m \right\rbrack Y\quad \Rightarrow \quad Y^{*} = \frac{A}{1 - c(1 - \tau) + m}\quad(\#e44)$$

$Y^{*}$ is the level of national income at which planned spending equals output. Figure {fig:kcross} draws it: planned expenditure rises with income along a line of slope $c(1 - \tau) - m$, and equilibrium is where that line crosses the 45-degree line on which spending equals output. Below $Y^{*}$ planned spending exceeds output, stocks run down and firms expand; above it the reverse. The response of the equilibrium to a change in autonomous spending is

$$k = \frac{dY^{*}}{dA} = \frac{1}{1 - c(1 - \tau) + m}\quad(\#e45)$$

![Figure {fig:kcross}. The Keynesian cross. Equilibrium income is where planned expenditure meets the 45-degree line. A rise in autonomous spending of $\Delta A$ raises income by $k\Delta A$, more than the original injection.](figs/kcross.png)

The denominator is the fraction of each extra pound of national income that leaks out of the circular flow. Splitting it shows where the leaks go:

$$1 - c(1 - \tau) + m = \underset{\text{MPS}}{\underbrace{(1 - c)(1 - \tau)}} + \underset{\text{MPT}}{\underbrace{\tau}} + \underset{\text{MPM}}{\underbrace{m}}\quad(\#empw)$$

Of each extra pound of national income, $\tau$ is taxed, a fraction $1 - c$ of the remaining $1 - \tau$ is saved, and $m$ is spent abroad. So ({eq:e45}) is the familiar $k = 1/(MPS + MPT + MPM) = 1/MPW$: the multiplier is the reciprocal of the withdrawal rate.

Suppose $G$ rises by £1bn on a railway. That pound is income to construction firms and their workers. They pay tax on it, save some, and spend some on imports; what remains, $z = c(1 - \tau) - m$ per pound, is spent on domestic goods and becomes somebody else's income, who repeats the process on a smaller sum. The rounds of spending are $1, z, z^{2}, z^{3}, \ldots$, so the total income created by £1 of injection is the geometric series $1 + z + z^{2} + \cdots = 1/(1 - z)$, which is ({eq:e45}), and which converges because $0 < z < 1$. With $c = 0.8$, $\tau = 0.25$ and $m = 0.15$, the withdrawal rate is $0.15 + 0.25 + 0.15 = 0.55$ and the multiplier is $1/0.55 = 1.82$, so £1bn of spending eventually raises income by about £1.8bn.

Aggregate demand is the same total expenditure viewed as a function of the price level. A rise in the price level is not a fall in any good's relative price: if every price rose together nothing would become cheaper than anything else, so the substitution argument of sections {sec:s1} and {sec:s7} cannot be borrowed. Three other mechanisms make autonomous spending depend on the price level. A lower price level raises the **real value of money-denominated wealth**, so consumption rises. It reduces the money needed for transactions, which puts downward pressure on **interest rates**, so investment and durable consumption rise. And it makes domestic goods more **competitive** against foreign ones at a given nominal exchange rate, so net exports rise. Writing autonomous spending as $A(P)$ with $A'(P) < 0$, equation ({eq:e44}) becomes the aggregate demand curve:

$$Y = k A(P)\quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{dY}{dP} = k A'(P) < 0\quad(\#ead)$$

AD slopes downward because each of the three channels lowers autonomous spending as the price level rises, and the multiplier amplifies each pound of that fall. A larger multiplier therefore makes AD flatter: the same rise in prices removes more output.

![Figure {fig:f24}. Aggregate demand, $Y = kA(P)$. A lower price level raises autonomous spending through real wealth, interest rates and net exports, and the multiplier amplifies the effect.](figs/f24.png)

A shift of AD is any change in $C$, $I$, $G$ or $X - M$ **not** caused by the price level: consumer confidence, interest-rate decisions, fiscal policy, world income, the exchange rate. At any given price level, a rise in autonomous spending of $\Delta A$ raises the output demanded by $k\Delta A$, so AD moves right by the multiplier times the change in autonomous spending; seen vertically, the same move is an upward shift, and section {sec:s20} calls it demand-pull. This is the one curve whose shift is easier to measure sideways than vertically.

Investment is the most volatile component of demand, and the accelerator is part of the reason. If firms want a capital stock proportional to output, investment depends on the **change** in output rather than its level. When output growth merely slows, the capital stock needed grows more slowly, so investment falls in absolute terms even though output is still rising. Investment therefore swings more than output and amplifies the cycle.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The multiplier in ({eq:e45}) assumes output can actually expand, that is, spare capacity and idle labour. With the economy already at capacity the extra demand cannot raise real output and raises prices instead, and the real multiplier collapses towards zero. Section {sec:s19} makes that explicit.\
> The propensities $c$, $\tau$ and $m$ are assumed constant. In a recession, when households want to rebuild savings, $c$ can fall just as government spending rises.
