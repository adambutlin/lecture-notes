## Oligopoly and monopolistic competition {#s12b}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Oligopoly**: a few firms large enough that each must anticipate the others' reactions.\
> **Monopolistic competition**: many firms selling differentiated products with free entry.\
> **Concentration ratio**: the combined market share of the largest $n$ firms.\
> **Collusion**: firms agreeing, formally or tacitly, to restrict competition; a formal agreement is a **cartel**.\
> **Dominant strategy**: a choice that is best whatever rivals do.\
> **Nash equilibrium**: a set of choices in which no firm can do better by changing its own choice, given the others'.\
> **Contestable market**: one in which entry and exit are cheap, so conduct is competitive whatever the number of firms.

In oligopoly the demand curve a firm faces depends on what its rivals do, so the firm cannot work out its best price without forming a view of their response. Interdependence rather than concentration alone is the defining feature.

The kinked demand model makes one assumption about that response: rivals match price cuts, to avoid losing customers, but ignore price rises, happy to take customers from the firm that raised its price. Above the current price the firm's demand is then much more elastic than below it, and marginal revenue jumps downward at the kink. Marginal cost can move anywhere within that gap without changing the profit-maximising price, which is the model's prediction of sticky prices and of competition through branding, quality and advertising instead. The lower segment is **less** elastic than the upper one, not inelastic: by ({eq:e33}), a firm maximising profit never sits where $|PED| < 1$, and in Figure {fig:f18} marginal revenue just to the right of the kink is still positive.

Game theory treats the interdependence directly. Two firms each choose a high or a low price, and each cell of the table gives their weekly profits in £m, firm A's first:

  -------------------------------------------------------------------
                         Firm B: high price     Firm B: low price
  ---------------------- ---------------------- ---------------------
  Firm A: high price     10, 10                 4, 14

  Firm A: low price      14, 4                  6, 6
  -------------------------------------------------------------------

Whatever B does, A earns more by pricing low: 14 rather than 10 if B prices high, 6 rather than 4 if B prices low. Low is a dominant strategy for A, and by symmetry for B, so both price low and earn 6 each. That outcome is a Nash equilibrium, since neither can gain by changing its own price alone, and it is worse for both than the 10 each they would earn by pricing high. This structure, in which individually rational choices produce a jointly bad result, is the **prisoners' dilemma**, and it is why collusion is both tempting and unstable.

Repetition changes the calculation. Suppose the game is played every week and each firm prices high for as long as the other does, but switches to low for ever once the other cheats. Cheating earns 14 once and 6 every week after; cooperating earns 10 every week. If a firm values £1 next week at $\delta$ pounds today, cooperation pays when

$$\frac{10}{1 - \delta} \geq 14 + \frac{6\delta}{1 - \delta}\quad \Rightarrow \quad 10 \geq 14(1 - \delta) + 6\delta\quad \Rightarrow \quad \delta \geq 0.5\quad(\#egame)$$

The left-hand side sums $10 + 10\delta + 10\delta^{2} + \cdots$; the right-hand side is 14 now plus 6 in every later week. Collusion survives when firms care enough about the future relative to the one-off gain from cheating, and when cheating is quickly detected. That is why cartels need monitoring, why they form in markets with few firms and transparent prices, and why competition authorities offer leniency to whichever member confesses first: the offer turns the cartel itself into a prisoners' dilemma.

Monopolistic competition sits between monopoly and perfect competition. Differentiation gives each firm a downward-sloping demand curve, so $P > MC$, but free entry competes supernormal profit away in the long run. Each firm ends where its demand curve just touches its average cost curve, on the falling part of that curve, so it produces less than the output that would minimise average cost: the industry carries excess capacity.

  -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
                          Perfect competition   Monopolistic competition   Oligopoly            Monopoly
  ----------------------- --------------------- -------------------------- -------------------- ---------------------
  Firms                   Very many             Many                       Few                  One

  Product                 Identical             Differentiated             Either               Unique

  Entry barriers          None                  Low                        High                 High

  Firm's demand           Horizontal            Elastic, downward          Interdependent       Market demand

  Long-run profit         Normal                Normal                     Can be supernormal   Supernormal

  Allocative efficiency   Yes, $P = MC$         No, $P > MC$               No, $P > MC$         No, $P > MC$ by the mark-up in ({eq:e33})

  Productive efficiency   Yes, at $\min ATC$    No, excess capacity        Not guaranteed       No
  -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dynamic efficiency often runs the opposite way to the static measures in the table: the protected profits that make monopoly inefficient are also what can fund research. The number of firms can also mislead. A contestable market behaves competitively however few firms are actually in it, because the threat of entry does the work that rivalry would otherwise do.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The kinked demand curve assumes rivals match cuts and ignore rises, which explains why a price is sticky once set but not how it was set in the first place.\
> The game assumes each firm knows the payoffs and that the other is rational. The repeated version also assumes the game has no known last week; if it did, cheating would pay in the last week, and so, working backwards, in every week.
