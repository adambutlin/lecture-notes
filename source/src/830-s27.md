## Protection and integration {#s27}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Tariff**: a tax on imports.\
> **Quota**: a legal limit on the quantity imported.\
> **Producer subsidy**: a payment per unit to domestic producers.\
> **Quota rent**: the gap between the world price and the domestic price on the units allowed in, which goes to whoever holds the licence.\
> **Production distortion**: the loss from making at home what could have been bought more cheaply abroad.\
> **Consumption distortion**: the loss from units buyers valued above their cost and no longer buy.\
> **Terms of trade index**: 100 times the index of export prices divided by the index of import prices.\
> **Trade creation**: a shift from higher-cost domestic production to lower-cost imports from a trading-bloc partner; **trade diversion**: a shift from lower-cost imports from outside the bloc to higher-cost imports from inside it.

Take a country small enough that it cannot move the world price, so it can buy or sell any quantity at $P_{w}$. Domestic demand and domestic supply are the curves of sections {sec:s7} and {sec:s8}, and the gap between them at the world price is filled from abroad:

$$\text{imports} = Q_{D}\left( P_{w} \right) - Q_{S}\left( P_{w} \right)\quad(\#e53)$$

If the world price were above the domestic equilibrium price the sign would flip and the country would export instead.

Suppose domestic demand is $P = 20 - 0.1Q$ and domestic supply $P = 4 + 0.1Q$, which would clear at a price of 12 without trade. The world price is 8. At that price domestic firms supply 40, domestic buyers want 120, and 80 units are imported.

A tariff is an indirect tax on imports, so section {sec:s14} applies: the supply of imports moves **up** by the tariff, and since that supply is perfectly elastic at the world price, the whole tariff lands on the buyer. With $t = 2$ the domestic price rises to 10. Domestic production rises from 40 to 60, consumption falls from 120 to 100, and imports are squeezed from 80 to 40. The government collects $2 \times 40 = 80$.

A quota capping imports at 40 produces the identical price and the identical quantities. Only the shaded rectangle differs. Under the tariff it is government revenue; under the quota it is the gap between the world price and the domestic price on 40 imported units, and it goes to whoever holds the licence to import them. The state raises nothing, which is one reason quotas are harder to defend than tariffs.

![Figure {fig:f34}. A tariff and a quota. The price effect is the same; only the destination of the shaded rectangle differs.](figs/f34.png)

Both grey triangles are genuine losses rather than transfers. The left one is a production distortion: units 40 to 60 are now made domestically at a marginal cost above 8 when they could have been bought for 8. The right one is a consumption distortion: units 100 to 120 were worth more to buyers than the 8 they cost, and are no longer bought. Each has area $\frac{1}{2} \times 20 \times 2 = 20$, so the tariff destroys 40 in total.

A producer subsidy reaches the same domestic output by a different route. Paying domestic firms 2 per unit moves domestic supply **down** by 2, so at the unchanged world price they now supply 60. Consumers are untouched: the price is still 8 and consumption is still 120, so imports fall to 60 rather than to 40. The cost is $2 \times 60 = 120$, paid by taxpayers, and the only welfare loss is the production distortion of 20. The consumption distortion never arises, because nobody was priced out of the market.

![Figure {fig:f35}. A producer subsidy. Domestic output rises as far as the tariff pushed it, but consumers pay the world price throughout.](figs/f35.png)

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
                       Tariff of 2            Quota of 40             Subsidy of 2
  -------------------- ---------------------- ----------------------- --------------------
  Price to consumers   10                     10                      8, unchanged

  Domestic output      60                     60                      60

  Consumption          100                    100                     120, unchanged

  Imports              40                     40                      60

  Transfer             80 to the government   80 to licence holders   120 from taxpayers

  Welfare loss         40                     40                      20
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

If the objective is to support domestic producers, the subsidy achieves the same domestic output as the tariff at half the welfare loss, because it distorts only the production margin and leaves the consumption margin alone. Its cost also appears in the Budget rather than being hidden in shop prices.

Countries also lower barriers selectively, by joining trading blocs. In ascending order of integration, a **preferential trade agreement** cuts tariffs on some goods between members; a **free trade area** removes them on trade between members while each keeps its own tariffs on outsiders; a **customs union** adds a common external tariff; a **common market** adds free movement of labour and capital; and a **monetary union** adds a single currency and so a single monetary policy.

The tariff example shows why joining a bloc can lower welfare. Keep the tariff of 2 on imports from the rest of the world, whose price is 8, and form a customs union with a partner whose cost is 9. The partner's goods now enter tariff-free at 9, cheaper than the world's 8 plus the tariff, so every import switches to the partner, and the domestic price falls from 10 to 9. Two things happen. The lower price creates trade: domestic production falls to 50 and consumption rises to 110, removing parts of both distortion triangles worth $\frac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 1 = 5$ each. But the 40 units the country already imported now come from a producer whose cost is 9 rather than 8, which wastes 1 on each of them, a trade-diversion loss of 40. Net welfare falls by 30. Had the partner's cost been 8.5, trade creation would have been worth 22.5 and diversion would have cost 20, a small net gain. A bloc raises welfare when it mainly replaces costly domestic production and lowers it when it mainly replaces cheaper imports from outside.

The terms of trade measure what a country's exports buy:

$$\text{terms of trade} = 100 \times \frac{\text{index of export prices}}{\text{index of import prices}}\quad(\#etot)$$

If export prices rise by 10 per cent while import prices rise by 20 per cent, the index falls to $100 \times 110/120 = 91.7$: each unit of exports now buys about 8 per cent fewer imports, a deterioration. A depreciation worsens the terms of trade in this way, since it raises import prices in sterling, which is the "minus one" in ({eq:e51}). Countries that export a few primary commodities face large swings in their terms of trade whenever commodity prices move, which section {sec:sdev} takes up.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The country is small enough to take the world price as given, so none of the burden is pushed onto foreign exporters; a large country's tariff would also move the world price and capture some of it.\
> There is no retaliation, no infant industry that grows competitive behind the barrier, and no strategic or security value in domestic production. The arguments for protection that survive scrutiny all work by attacking one of these assumptions, which is why they have to be argued rather than asserted.\
> The customs union example looks only at one market; blocs also bring larger markets, more competition and economies of scale, which a single-market calculation leaves out.
