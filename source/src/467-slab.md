## Labour markets {#slab}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Derived demand**: demand for an input that exists because of demand for what it produces.\
> **Marginal revenue product of labour ($MRP_{L}$)**: the extra revenue from employing one more worker, equal to marginal revenue times the marginal product of labour.\
> **Marginal cost of labour ($MCL$)**: the addition to the wage bill from employing one more worker.\
> **Monopsony**: a market with a single buyer, here a single employer.\
> **National Minimum Wage**: a legal floor on hourly pay; the **National Living Wage** is its rate for workers aged 21 and over.\
> **Wage differentials**: differences in pay between jobs or workers, reflecting differences in productivity, skill, the attractiveness of the work, and bargaining power.

Section {sec:s6} chose output to maximise profit. Choosing employment is the same problem seen through the input. With capital fixed at $K_{0}$, output is $F(K_{0}, L)$, and profit as a function of the number of workers is

$$\Pi(L) = TR\big(F(K_{0}, L)\big) - wL - RK_{0}\quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{d\Pi}{dL} = MR \times MP_{L} - w = 0\quad \Rightarrow \quad MRP_{L} = w\quad(\#emrp)$$

The chain rule splits the revenue from one more worker into the extra output that worker produces, $MP_{L}$, and the revenue each extra unit brings, $MR$. The firm hires until the last worker adds as much to revenue as to costs. For a price taker $MR = P$, and the condition $P \times MP_{L} = w$ is ({eq:e12})'s $P = MC = w/MP_{L}$ rearranged: hiring the right number of workers and producing the right quantity of output are one decision, not two.

Diminishing returns make $MP_{L}$, and so $MRP_{L}$, fall as employment rises, so the $MRP_{L}$ curve is the firm's demand curve for labour and slopes downward. It shifts with anything that changes either factor: a higher price for the product, or better capital and training that raise each worker's marginal product. Demand for labour is derived demand. Labour supply slopes upward because a higher wage draws people into the labour force, into longer hours and away from other occupations. In a competitive labour market the wage settles where the two meet, and differences in wages between occupations reflect differences in the marginal revenue product of the work and in how many people are able and willing to do it.

A minimum wage set above that competitive wage is a price floor of the kind section {sec:s14} analyses. Employers move up their demand curves and hire fewer workers, while more people want work at the higher wage; the gap between the two is unemployment.

![Figure {fig:labmin}. A competitive labour market. The wage settles where the marginal revenue product curve meets labour supply; a minimum wage above that level reduces employment and creates unemployment.](figs/labmin.png)

That prediction depends on the employer being a wage taker. A monopsonist, the only significant employer in a town, faces the whole upward-sloping supply curve, so hiring one more worker means raising the wage for everyone already employed. Suppose labour supply is $w = 4 + 0.05L$ (pounds per hour) and $MRP_{L} = 20 - 0.1L$. The wage bill is $wL = 4L + 0.05L^{2}$, so

$$MCL = \frac{d(wL)}{dL} = 4 + 0.1L\quad(\#emcl)$$

This is section {sec:s5}'s marginal revenue argument run on the buying side: the extra worker costs their own wage plus the rise paid to everyone else. Setting $MRP_{L} = MCL$ gives $20 - 0.1L = 4 + 0.1L$, so the monopsonist employs 80 workers and pays only what the supply curve requires for 80, $w = 4 + 0.05 \times 80 = £8$. A competitive market would have set $MRP_{L}$ equal to the supply price: $20 - 0.1L = 4 + 0.05L$, giving 106.7 workers at £9.33. The monopsonist hires fewer people and pays them less.

A minimum wage now has the opposite effect to the competitive case. At a legal floor of £9.33, the employer can hire anyone willing to work at that wage without raising pay for the others, so its marginal cost of labour is flat at £9.33 up to the supply curve, and it hires 106.7 workers: employment and pay both rise. Any floor between £8 and £12, the marginal revenue product at the monopsony employment of 80, leaves employment at least as high as without it. This is why the evidence that moderate minimum wages have reduced employment little is not a puzzle: much low-wage employment is in markets where employers have some power over pay.

![Figure {fig:monops}. Monopsony. The employer sets $MRP_{L} = MCL$, hires 80 workers and pays the supply price of £8. A minimum wage of £9.33 makes the marginal cost of labour flat and raises employment to the competitive level.](figs/monops.png)

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Workers are paid their marginal revenue product only where the employer takes the wage as given and can measure what each worker adds.\
> Trade unions are a monopoly on the selling side of the labour market. Facing a monopsonist, a union can raise both pay and employment by the same mechanism as the minimum wage; facing competitive employers, it raises pay at the cost of jobs.\
> The figures in the monopsony example are illustrative. The range over which a minimum wage raises employment depends on how much power employers really have.
