## The long run and economies of scale {#s4}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Returns to scale**: what happens to output when every input is increased in the same proportion.\
> **Economies of scale**: falling long-run average cost as the scale of production rises.\
> **Diseconomies of scale**: rising long-run average cost, usually from coordination and motivation problems.\
> **Minimum efficient scale**: the lowest output at which long-run average cost stops falling.\
> **Natural monopoly**: an industry in which minimum efficient scale is so large relative to demand that one firm produces more cheaply than several could.

In the long run nothing is fixed, so there are no fixed costs and there is no law of diminishing returns to appeal to. If doubling $K$ and $L$ more than doubles $Q$, the firm has **increasing returns to scale** and long-run average cost falls; if it exactly doubles $Q$, constant returns and flat $LRAC$; if less, decreasing returns and rising $LRAC$.

Each conceivable plant size generates its own short-run $ATC$ curve. The firm choosing optimally will always sit on the lowest one available, so the long-run curve is the lower envelope of all of them.

Economies of scale come from identifiable sources: indivisible equipment that only pays at volume (technical), bulk discounts on inputs (purchasing), specialised managers whose salary is spread over more output (managerial), cheaper borrowing for large and safer firms (financial), and advertising whose cost per unit sold falls with sales (marketing). Diseconomies come mostly from coordination and motivation: more layers of management, slower information, weaker individual accountability.

A supermarket and a corner shop sell similar goods with sharply different unit costs. The supermarket runs a distribution centre, negotiates directly with manufacturers, and spreads a single logistics system over thousands of stores. The corner shop cannot do any of this at its volume. When minimum efficient scale, MES for short, is large relative to total market demand, the market cannot support many firms at efficient size, and the structure of the industry is decided by the cost curve rather than by anybody's strategy. In the extreme, where MES exceeds the whole market, one firm is the cheapest possible arrangement and the industry is a natural monopoly.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Every input can be varied, and the firm picks whichever plant size makes each output cheapest, which is what places it on the lower envelope.\
> Technology and input prices are held fixed while scale changes.\
> The sources of economies of scale are empirical claims about particular industries rather than consequences of the model, and the upturn of the long-run curve is an assumption about where diseconomies eventually set in.
