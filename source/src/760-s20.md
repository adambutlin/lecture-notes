## Inflation and unemployment {#s20}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Inflation**: a sustained rise in the general price level.\
> **Deflation**: a sustained fall.\
> **Disinflation**: a fall in the rate of inflation while prices are still rising.\
> **Consumer Prices Index**: the cost of a fixed, expenditure-weighted basket of goods, repriced each month.\
> **Demand-pull inflation**: caused by aggregate demand rising, so output rises too.\
> **Cost-push inflation**: caused by input costs rising, so output falls at the same time.\
> **Unemployment**: people out of work who are available for and seeking work; the **unemployment rate** is their number as a share of the labour force.\
> **NAIRU**: the unemployment rate consistent with stable inflation.

Inflation is a statement about a rate of change, not a level. The UK measure is the Consumer Prices Index: a fixed basket of goods, weighted by how much households actually spend on each, repriced each month. Falling inflation is not falling prices; that is deflation, a different condition. The index has known weaknesses: it holds the basket fixed while consumers substitute away from what is rising, it struggles with quality improvements and new products, and CPI excludes owner-occupied housing costs, which is why CPIH exists.

Sections {sec:s17} and {sec:s18} give two ways the price level can rise. **Demand-pull** inflation is an upward shift of AD: output and prices both rise, and it happens when the economy is at or near capacity. **Cost-push** inflation is an upward shift of SRAS caused by higher input costs, each unit of output now needing a higher price to be worth making: prices rise while output falls, which is stagflation and is the harder case for policy because the two targets pull in opposite directions.

![Figure {fig:f27}. The two sources of inflation. Only cost-push reduces output at the same time.](figs/f27.png)

The UK experience from 2021 to 2023 was mostly the second. Wholesale gas prices rose several-fold after supply disruption, energy is an input to almost everything, so unit costs rose across the economy and SRAS moved up. Raising interest rates could not produce more gas. It could only reduce demand, which is why that episode involved an explicit trade of lost output against lower inflation rather than a free correction.

Money gives a third account of the price level. The equation of exchange is

$$M_{S}V \equiv PY\quad(\#e47)$$

The money supply $M_{S}$, multiplied by the **velocity of circulation** $V$, the number of times each pound is spent in a year, must equal the total value of what was bought, the price level $P$ times real output $Y$. Like ({eq:e41}) it is an identity, true by the definition of $V$, and it predicts nothing on its own. The **quantity theory** adds two behavioural assumptions: that $V$ is stable and that $Y$ is fixed at capacity. Only then does $M_{S}$ rising by 10 per cent imply $P$ rising by 10 per cent. Both assumptions are contested, and $V$ moved sharply in the UK after 2009, so the monetarist conclusion is only as strong as those assumptions.

The UK measures unemployment two ways: the claimant count, which depends on benefit rules, and the Labour Force Survey using the ILO definition, which is the internationally comparable one. Neither captures underemployment or the economically inactive. The remedy follows from the cause:

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Type                Cause                                           What actually helps
  ------------------- ----------------------------------------------- ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Cyclical            a negative output gap                           demand-side policy

  Structural          skills or location no longer match the jobs     training, mobility, regional policy

  Frictional          normal churn between jobs                       better matching and information

  Real-wage           the wage held above its market-clearing level   wage flexibility, though not where employers have monopsony power (section {sec:slab})

  Seasonal            predictable annual swings in demand             little, beyond smoothing incomes across the year
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The **Phillips curve** began as an empirical relationship: low unemployment came with high inflation. The modern version adds expectations:

$$\pi = \pi^{e} - \omega\left( u - u_{n} \right)\quad(\#ephil)$$

$\pi$ is inflation, $\pi^{e}$ is the inflation that wage and price setters expect, $u$ is the unemployment rate, $u_{n}$ is the NAIRU, and $\omega > 0$ measures how strongly a tight labour market bids up wages and prices. With $\omega = 0.5$ and expected inflation of 2 per cent, holding unemployment one point below the NAIRU gives inflation of 2.5 per cent this year. That is the short-run trade-off, and it is real. But once inflation has run at 2.5 per cent, people come to expect it; with $\pi^{e} = 2.5$, the same unemployment rate now brings 3 per cent inflation, and so on. Keeping unemployment below the NAIRU requires ever-rising inflation.

In the long run expectations are correct, $\pi = \pi^{e}$, and ({eq:ephil}) then forces $u = u_{n}$: whatever the inflation rate, unemployment returns to the NAIRU. Each short-run Phillips curve is drawn for one value of $\pi^{e}$, and a rise in expectations shifts it up.

![Figure {fig:f28}. Short-run Phillips curves, each drawn for given expected inflation, and the vertical long-run curve at the NAIRU, where expectations are correct.](figs/f28.png)

That vertical long-run curve is the Phillips-diagram version of vertical LRAS, and the NAIRU is determined by the structure of the labour market rather than by demand. Inflation is costly through menu and shoe-leather costs, through arbitrary redistribution from lenders to borrowers when it is unanticipated, through the uncertainty it creates for investment, and through lost competitiveness if it exceeds trading partners' inflation. Deflation has costs of its own: it raises the real burden of debt, and when it comes from collapsing demand it gives buyers a reason to delay purchases, which weakens demand further. Deflation that comes from rapid productivity growth, as with computers and phones, lowers prices because goods have become cheaper to make, and carries none of those dangers.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The Phillips curve trade-off assumes expectations adjust slowly; if wage and price setters anticipated inflation at once, $\pi^{e}$ would equal $\pi$ immediately and there would be no short-run trade-off to exploit.\
> The quantity theory assumes a stable velocity and output fixed at capacity, and neither holds reliably.\
> The CPI assumes a fixed basket, so it overstates the rise in the cost of living when households switch away from goods whose prices are rising.
