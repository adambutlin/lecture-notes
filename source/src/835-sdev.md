## Growth and development {#sdev}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Economic development**: a broad improvement in living standards, including health, education and the freedom to make choices, as well as income.\
> **Human Development Index (HDI)**: a composite of life expectancy, education and income per head, each scaled between 0 and 1.\
> **Poverty trap**: a situation in which low income itself prevents the saving and investment needed to escape low income.\
> **Primary product dependency**: reliance on a few agricultural or mineral exports.\
> **Foreign direct investment (FDI)**: investment by a foreign firm in productive assets, such as a factory, in another country.\
> **Official development assistance**: aid from governments, as grants or loans on favourable terms.

Growth is a rise in real GDP; development is a rise in living standards more broadly, and the two can diverge. A country can grow quickly while its gains go to a few, or while life expectancy and schooling stagnate. Section {sec:s16} showed that comparing incomes across countries needs PPP. The Human Development Index goes further and combines three dimensions:

$$\text{HDI} = \left( I_{\text{health}} \times I_{\text{education}} \times I_{\text{income}} \right)^{1/3}\quad(\#ehdi)$$

Each index runs from 0 to 1: life expectancy, years of schooling, and the logarithm of income per head at PPP, each measured against fixed minimum and maximum values. The HDI is their geometric mean, the cube root of the product, rather than their average, and that choice has economic content. With an average, a shortfall in health could be fully offset by more income. With a geometric mean it cannot: a country with indices of 0.9, 0.9 and 0.3 has an average of 0.7 but an HDI of $(0.9 \times 0.9 \times 0.3)^{1/3} = 0.62$, so uneven development is penalised. The income index uses the logarithm because each extra pound matters less to a rich country than to a poor one, the diminishing marginal utility of section {sec:s1} applied to nations.

The barriers to development are the failures of earlier sections at a national scale. Low incomes leave little to save, so investment and the capital stock stay low, and with them income: a poverty trap in which section {sec:s15}'s choice between consumption now and capacity later is not really available. Poor health and limited schooling hold down the productivity of labour. Weak institutions, insecure property rights and corruption deter the investment that growth accounting ({eq:egrowth}) says is needed, and lower the efficiency captured by TFP. Dependence on primary products exposes export earnings to the terms-of-trade swings of section {sec:s27}, and borrowing in foreign currency turns a depreciation into a heavier debt burden.

Strategies divide roughly as the supply-side policies of section {sec:s24} did. Market-oriented strategies emphasise openness to trade, so that resources follow comparative advantage as in section {sec:s26}, export-led growth, FDI that brings capital and technology together, microfinance that gives small borrowers access to credit, and secure property rights. Interventionist strategies emphasise public investment in infrastructure, health and education, protection of infant industries, and aid. The fastest-growing economies of the last half-century, in East Asia, combined heavy investment in education with openness to export markets, which is one reason the debate between the two approaches is less sharp than its labels suggest. Aid can fill the savings gap and fund health and schooling, but it can also support governments that would otherwise reform, and its effects are hard to measure.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The HDI's weights and its limits for each dimension are choices, not facts, and different choices rank countries differently.\
> The poverty trap assumes saving depends on current income; access to foreign capital through FDI, borrowing or aid can break the link.
