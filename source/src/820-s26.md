## Trade and comparative advantage {#s26}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Absolute advantage**: producing more of a good with the same resources.\
> **Comparative advantage**: producing a good at a lower opportunity cost, which is what determines the pattern of trade and has nothing to do with being better at anything.\
> **Specialisation**: concentrating resources on the good in which the comparative advantage lies.\
> **Terms of trade**: the rate at which one good exchanges for another, which must lie between the two opportunity costs if both sides are to gain.

Trade follows comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, and comparative advantage is the opportunity cost of section {sec:s15} applied across countries. Suppose that with the same resources the UK can make 60 loaves or 30 shirts, while Portugal can make 40 loaves or 40 shirts. Portugal makes fewer loaves and more shirts than the UK in absolute terms, but the relevant numbers are the ratios:

$$\text{UK: }1\text{ shirt costs }2\text{ loaves};\quad\quad\text{Portugal: }1\text{ shirt costs }1\text{ loaf}\quad(\#e52)$$

Each figure is what a country gives up to make one shirt: the UK sacrifices $60/30 = 2$ loaves, Portugal $40/40 = 1$. These are opportunity costs in the sense of ({eq:e40}), applied to countries, and in those terms shirts are cheaper in Portugal and loaves are cheaper in the UK.

The UK makes more loaves, 60 against 40, so it has the absolute advantage in loaves. Portugal makes more shirts, 40 against 30, so Portugal has the absolute advantage in shirts. In this example the comparative advantage happens to point the same way, and that is a coincidence of these particular numbers rather than a general rule. Suppose instead that Portugal could make 100 loaves or 40 shirts. It would still hold an absolute advantage in shirts, and now in loaves too, but a shirt would cost it 2.5 loaves against the UK's 2, so its comparative advantage would have moved to loaves and the UK's to shirts. Specialisation would run the other way round. What a country makes more of tells you nothing about what it should make; only what it gives up to make it does.

Portugal can make a shirt for 1 loaf but sell it for 1.5 loaves. The UK can only make a shirt at a cost of 2 loaves but can buy one for 1.5. Each country is getting the good it is worse at making for less than making it would cost, which is why the trading rate has to sit between the two opportunity costs, and why the gain disappears if the opportunity costs are the same.

Suppose both countries begin by splitting their resources evenly between the two goods, then let each move resources towards the good it gives up least to make, then let them trade 24 loaves for 16 shirts, which is the rate of 1.5 loaves per shirt:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
                                              Loaves              Shirts
  ------------------------------------------- ------------------- -------------------
  **Without trade, resources split evenly**                       

  UK produces and consumes                    30                  15

  Portugal produces and consumes              20                  20

  World total                                 50                  35

  **After specialising**                                          

  UK, now making only loaves                  60                  0

  Portugal, now making only shirts            0                   40

  World total                                 60                  40

  **After trading 24 loaves for 16 shirts**                       

  UK consumes                                 36                  16

  Portugal consumes                           24                  24
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Total output rises: the world ends up with 60 loaves rather than 50 and 40 shirts rather than 35, from the same workers, land and machines as before. The extra output comes only from each country's resources being pointed at what that country sacrifices least to produce.

The UK ends with 36 loaves and 16 shirts against 30 and 15 without trade, so it has more of both. Portugal ends with 24 and 24 against 20 and 20, so it also has more of both. Neither country has given anything up, and no third party paid for it. That is why the case for trade does not rest on one country being better at anything.

Complete specialisation is not required for any of this. The gain appears as soon as resources move in the right direction, so partial specialisation delivers a smaller version of the same result. Straight-line frontiers are what push the example all the way to complete specialisation; with the bowed frontier of section {sec:s15}, the opportunity cost of loaves would rise as the UK made more of them, and specialisation would stop at the point where the two countries' opportunity costs had converged on the trading rate.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Constant opportunity costs, so both frontiers are straight lines.\
> No transport costs.\
> Factors mobile between industries within a country but not between countries.\
> Full employment, so the resources one industry releases are absorbed by another.\
> Relax the last two and the model still shows an aggregate gain, but the adjustment falls on particular workers in particular places, which is why the politics of trade is harder than its economics.
