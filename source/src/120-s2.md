## Production and diminishing returns {#s2}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Production function**: the relationship between the quantities of inputs used and the maximum output obtainable from them.\
> **Short run**: a period in which at least one input cannot be varied.\
> **Long run**: a period in which every input can be varied.\
> **Marginal product of labour**: the extra output produced by one more worker, capital held fixed.\
> **Average product of labour**: output divided by the number of workers.\
> **Law of diminishing marginal returns**: with at least one factor fixed, the marginal product of the variable factor eventually falls.

The cost of making one more unit depends first on technology, which the production function describes:

$$Q = F(K,L)\quad(\#e8)$$

$Q$ is output per period, $K$ is capital (premises, machines, land), $L$ is labour, and $F$ is the recipe that turns the two into output. In the short run, take capital as the fixed input and write $K = K_{0}$, where the subscript marks a level that is held still rather than chosen, so that $Q = F\left( K_{0},L \right)$ and output depends on labour alone. The long run, when every input can vary, is the subject of section {sec:s4}.

Two measures describe the technology:

$$MP_{L} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial L},\quad\quad AP_{L} = \frac{Q}{L}\quad(\#e9)$$

The marginal product of labour $MP_{L}$ is the extra output the next worker adds, holding capital fixed; the average product $AP_{L}$ is output per worker across the whole workforce. The first is about the last person hired, the second about everybody.

Diminishing marginal returns means that with $K$ fixed, beyond some level of $L$ the marginal product falls: $\partial^{2}Q/\partial L^{2} < 0$. Take a bakery with one oven. The first extra baker is a large gain because one person cannot mix, shape, bake and serve at once. The fourth still helps. The tenth is queueing for oven space. Nothing has changed about the workers; what has changed is the capital each of them has to work with, which is why the law applies only in the short run.

The two products are linked by the quotient rule:

$$\frac{d}{dL}\left( \frac{Q}{L} \right) = \frac{L\,\frac{dQ}{dL} - Q}{L^{2}} = \frac{MP_{L} - AP_{L}}{L}\quad(\#e10)$$

The numerator compares the new worker's contribution with what the existing workers were producing on average, and since $L$ is positive the whole expression has the sign of $MP_{L} - AP_{L}$. So $AP_{L}$ rises while $MP_{L}$ is above it, falls while $MP_{L}$ is below it, and is stationary exactly where the two are equal: marginal cuts average at the average's maximum. The result is arithmetic rather than economics. It holds for any pair of marginal and average quantities, and it will settle the shape of the cost curves in section {sec:s3} and the meaning of a progressive tax in section {sec:sineq}.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> At least one factor fixed.\
> Workers identical and interchangeable.\
> Technology unchanged.\
> Output measured in physical units.\
> Diminishing returns is not a claim that later workers are lazier or less skilled. The tenth baker is as able as the first; what has fallen is the oven space each of them has.
