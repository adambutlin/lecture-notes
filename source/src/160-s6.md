## Profit maximisation {#s6}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Opportunity cost**: the value of the best alternative given up when a choice is made.\
> **Economic profit**: total revenue minus total cost, where cost includes the opportunity cost of every resource used.\
> **Normal profit**: the return just sufficient to keep the owner's resources in this use, which is counted as a cost and already sits inside total cost.\
> **Supernormal profit**: profit above normal profit.\
> **Shutdown point**: the price below which a firm stops producing in the short run, which is minimum average variable cost.\
> **Exit**: leaving the industry altogether, which a firm does in the long run if price stays below minimum average total cost.

Profit is the difference between two functions already derived. Writing it as capital $\Pi$ keeps the lower-case $\pi$ free for inflation in Part III:

$$\Pi(Q) = TR(Q) - TC(Q)\quad(\#e17)$$

The first-order condition for a maximum is

$$\frac{d\Pi}{dQ} = MR - MC = 0\quad \Rightarrow \quad MR = MC\quad(\#e18)$$

Differentiating ({eq:e17}) gives the extra revenue from the last unit minus its extra cost. If $MR > MC$, the next unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so making it raises profit and the firm should expand. If $MR < MC$, that unit is destroying profit and the firm should contract. Only where the two are equal is there nothing left to gain in either direction.

A turning point can be a maximum or a minimum, so the second-order condition checks which:

$$\frac{d^{2}\Pi}{dQ^{2}} = \frac{dMR}{dQ} - \frac{dMC}{dQ} < 0\quad(\#e19)$$

At the crossing, marginal cost must be rising faster than marginal revenue, so $MC$ must cut $MR$ **from below**. If marginal cost starts above marginal revenue and falls through it before rising back, there are two crossings, and the left-hand one, where $MC$ is still falling, satisfies $MR = MC$ but is a profit **minimum**.

A worked example makes the conditions concrete, and section {sec:s12} returns to the same firm. It faces demand $P = 14 - 0.06Q$, so $MR = 14 - 0.12Q$, and has marginal cost $MC = 6 - 0.12Q + 0.00105Q^{2}$, which falls to a minimum of £2.57 at $Q = 57$ and rises after that. Its fixed cost is £150. Setting $MR = MC$:

$$14 - 0.12Q = 6 - 0.12Q + 0.00105Q^{2}\quad \Rightarrow \quad 8 = 0.00105Q^{2}\quad \Rightarrow \quad Q^{*} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{0.00105}} \approx 87.3\quad(\#e32)$$

The $- 0.12Q$ terms cancel, which is a feature of these numbers rather than a general result. The second-order condition holds: $dMR/dQ = -0.12$ while $dMC/dQ = -0.12 + 0.0021 \times 87.3 = 0.063$, so marginal cost is rising through marginal revenue. The firm sells 87.3 units at $P = 14 - 0.06 \times 87.3 \approx £8.76$, average total cost there is £5.15, and profit is $(8.76 - 5.15) \times 87.3 \approx £316$.

The two curves in Figure {fig:f8}(a) have the shapes they do for economic reasons. Total revenue is price times quantity along a straight-line demand curve:

$$TR(Q) = (14 - 0.06Q)\,Q = 14Q - 0.06Q^{2}\quad(\#etrshape)$$

The negative coefficient on $Q^{2}$ makes it an upside-down parabola. At low output each extra unit adds almost its full price to revenue, but every extra unit sold needs a price cut on all the units already sold, and that loss grows with the number of units it applies to. Marginal revenue, $14 - 0.12Q$, therefore falls steadily: total revenue rises ever more slowly, peaks where $MR = 0$ at $Q = 116.7$, which by ({eq:e16}) is where $|PED| = 1$, and falls beyond it, where each further sale loses more on the existing units than it earns itself. The curve is symmetric about its peak because the loss term is a pure square, which in turn is because demand is a straight line.

Total cost is the fixed cost plus a variable cost whose slope is marginal cost:

$$TC(Q) = 150 + 6Q - 0.06Q^{2} + 0.00035Q^{3},\quad\quad\frac{d^{2}TC}{dQ^{2}} = -0.12 + 0.0021Q\quad(\#etcshape)$$

The curve starts at £150, the fixed cost paid even at zero output. Its gradient is marginal cost, which section {sec:s3} showed is the wage divided by the marginal product of labour. While extra workers make each other more productive, marginal cost falls, so total cost rises ever more slowly and the curve bends downward. Once diminishing returns set in, marginal cost rises, total cost rises ever more steeply, and the curve bends upward. The switch is the inflection point, where the second derivative is zero: $Q = 0.12/0.0021 = 57$, the output at which marginal cost is at its minimum. The S-shape of total cost is the U-shape of marginal cost seen in total form, and both are the product curves of section {sec:s2} turned upside down.

Profit is the vertical distance between the two curves, and the firm wants the output at which that distance is greatest. At that output the two curves must be parallel. If total revenue were steeper than total cost there, a little more output would add more to revenue than to cost and widen the gap; if total cost were steeper, a little less output would widen it. Only where the gradients are equal is there no direction in which the gap grows. The gradient of $TR$ is $MR$ and the gradient of $TC$ is $MC$, so parallel tangents are the condition $MR = MC$ drawn in totals. In Figure {fig:f8}(a) the tangents to both curves at $Q^{*} = 87.3$ have a slope of £3.53 per unit, which is the height at which $MR$ and $MC$ cross in panel (b).

Whether to produce at all is a separate question from how much. In the short run the fixed cost is sunk: it is paid whether or not the firm opens, so it cannot influence the decision. The firm should produce if revenue covers variable cost, $P \geq AVC$, and shut down if not. In the long run nothing is sunk, so a firm that cannot cover average total cost, $P < ATC$, exits the industry.

Firms also pursue other objectives. Revenue maximisation sets $MR = 0$, which in the example means $Q = 116.7$ at a price of £7: higher output and a lower price than profit maximisation. Managers may satisfice, or pursue growth, when ownership and control are separated.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The firm maximises profit, knows its own demand and cost functions, and chooses a single output.\
> Because normal profit sits inside total cost, a firm making zero economic profit is doing exactly as well as its next best alternative, not failing.
