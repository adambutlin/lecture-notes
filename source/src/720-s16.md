## National income and the circular flow {#s16}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Gross domestic product**: the value of everything produced in an economy in a period, measurable by output, by income or by expenditure.\
> **Nominal GDP** is valued at current prices and **real GDP** at the prices of a base year, so only real GDP measures output.\
> **GDP deflator**: nominal GDP divided by real GDP, times 100, an index of the price level.\
> **Purchasing power parity (PPP)**: an exchange rate at which the same basket of goods costs the same in both countries.\
> **Accounting identity**: a statement true by the definition of its terms, which therefore predicts nothing and cannot be tested.\
> **Gross national income**: the income earned by a country's residents, which is GDP plus income received from assets abroad minus income paid to foreign owners of domestic assets.\
> **Consumption**: household spending on goods and services.\
> **Investment**: spending on new capital goods, such as machinery, buildings and equipment, together with additions to firms' stocks of goods.\
> **Transfer payment**: a payment by the government for which no good or service is received in return, such as a pension or unemployment benefit; transfers are not part of government spending $G$.\
> **Withdrawals**: saving, taxes net of transfers, and imports.\
> **Injections**: investment, government spending on goods and services, and exports.

Everything produced in an economy is bought by a household, a firm, the government or a foreigner, so total output equals total expenditure on it:

$$Y \equiv C + I + G + (X - M)\quad(\#e41)$$

$Y$ is national output, also called national income; $C$ is household consumption; $I$ is investment, meaning purchases of new capital goods rather than anyone buying shares; $G$ is government spending on goods and services; $X$ is exports, what foreigners buy from us; and $M$ is imports, what we buy from abroad. The three-bar sign marks ({eq:e41}) as an accounting identity: it holds in every year, in every economy, under every policy, and it could not be false, so it predicts nothing.[^2] Imports are subtracted not because imports are bad but because $C$, $I$ and $G$ already include spending on foreign goods, which is not domestic output. $G$ excludes pensions and benefits, because paying them buys nothing: they move income from taxpayers to recipients, who then spend it as part of $C$.

The same total can be counted three ways, which is why GDP has an output measure, an income measure and an expenditure measure that agree by construction. The circular flow makes this visible: households supply factors and receive incomes, firms supply goods and receive spending, and the same money goes round. Not all income returns directly. Drawn in full, the economy has five sectors: households and firms form the core loop, and the financial sector, the government and the rest of the world each take money out of it and put money back in. National income is unchanging when

$$S + T + M = I + G + X\quad(\#e42)$$

Money leaves the circular flow when households save it ($S$), when the government takes it in tax ($T$), or when it is spent abroad ($M$), and money enters when firms invest ($I$), the government buys goods and services ($G$), or foreigners buy exports ($X$). While the two totals match, the flow of income is neither filling up nor draining. $T$ is taxes **net of transfers and subsidies**: a benefit paid to a household or a subsidy paid to a firm returns money the tax system took out, so it reduces the withdrawal rather than counting as an injection.

The rest of the world connects to the flow through trade, with import spending leaving and export revenue arriving. It also connects through ownership. When a UK household buys shares in a foreign company, or a foreign firm buys a factory in the UK, that purchase is an exchange of one asset for another and is not income, so it does not appear in a circular flow of income. What does appear is the income the ownership goes on to generate. UK residents who own assets abroad receive dividends and interest from the rest of the world, so overseas investment income flows in to households. Foreign owners of UK firms receive the profits those firms make, so those profits flow out of firms and offshore. Both are recorded in the primary income part of the current account, which section {sec:s25} takes up, and they are why gross national income, what a country's residents earn, can differ from gross domestic product, what is produced within its borders. Strictly, they add one more injection and one more withdrawal to ({eq:e42}). The A-level version leaves them out, which is harmless for most economies but misleading for one with heavy foreign ownership, where a large share of the profits generated at home is paid abroad.

Nominal GDP can rise because more is produced or because prices rose. Dividing by the GDP deflator removes the second, and for growth rates the correction is approximately a subtraction:

$$\text{real GDP} = \frac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{deflator}/100},\quad\quad g_{real} \approx g_{nominal} - \text{inflation}\quad(\#ereal)$$

If nominal GDP rises by 6 per cent while the deflator rises by 4 per cent, real output has grown by about 2 per cent (exactly $1.06/1.04 - 1 = 1.9$ per cent).

GDP per capita adjusts for population, which is the minimum correction before comparing countries, and comparing them also needs a common currency. Market exchange rates are set mainly by traded goods and capital flows, and in poorer countries non-traded services such as haircuts and rents are much cheaper, so converting at market rates understates what incomes buy. If a basket costing £100 in the UK costs ₹4,000 in India, the PPP rate is ₹40 per pound; at a market rate of ₹105 per pound, Indian incomes converted at market rates would look about 2.6 times poorer than they are in terms of what they buy. International comparisons of living standards therefore use GDP per capita at PPP.

GDP is a measure of production, not of welfare. It omits unpaid household work and the informal economy, says nothing about distribution, counts the cleanup of a disaster as output, and ignores environmental depletion. Section {sec:sdev} returns to broader measures.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The identity itself assumes nothing, which is both its strength and its limit.\
> The circular flow is simplified: each flow is one arrow between two sectors, when in reality every sector trades with every other, so the diagram shows the direction money moves rather than the full pattern of transactions. It draws the income that cross-border ownership produces but not the purchases of assets that created the ownership, because those are exchanges of wealth rather than flows of income.\
> Using GDP as a measure of welfare assumes market output is what matters.

[^2]: Equations ({eq:e41}) and ({eq:e42}) are definitions and cannot be tested. A statement such as "households spend a fixed fraction of disposable income" is a behavioural claim: it could be wrong, and it is what generates predictions. Almost all macroeconomic reasoning consists of feeding behavioural equations into an identity, so keeping track of which is which shows which parts of an argument could fail.
