## Fiscal policy {#s23}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Fiscal policy**: the use of government spending and taxation to affect aggregate demand.\
> **Automatic stabilisers**: features of the existing tax and benefit system that dampen the cycle with no decision being taken.\
> **Budget deficit**: the excess of spending over receipts in a year, a flow; the **primary deficit** excludes interest payments on the debt.\
> **National debt**: the accumulated total of past deficits, a stock.\
> **Structural deficit**: the part of the deficit that survives when the output gap closes.\
> **Crowding out**: the displacement of private spending by government borrowing or by government use of scarce resources.\
> **Laffer curve**: the relationship between a tax rate and the revenue it raises.

Fiscal policy acts on the same diagram through $G$ and, via disposable income, through $C$. Some of it happens without anyone deciding anything: in a downturn, tax receipts fall as incomes fall and benefit payments rise as unemployment rises, so disposable income falls by less than output does. These are the automatic stabilisers, and they are the reason the tax rate $\tau$ appears in the denominator of the multiplier ({eq:e45}): the higher it is, the more of any shock the budget absorbs. **Discretionary** policy is a deliberate change in rates or spending plans.

The deficit is a flow and the debt a stock. A falling deficit still adds to debt. The deficit also splits into a **cyclical** part, which disappears as the output gap closes, and a **structural** part, which does not, and only the structural part says anything about whether the fiscal position can be sustained.

The multiplier is the case for using fiscal policy in a slump, and the main objection to it is crowding out. Financial crowding out works through the market for loanable funds, where saving supplies funds and investment demands them, both depending on the real interest rate. Suppose saving is $S = 100 + 20r$ and investment is $I = 160 - 10r$, in £bn with $r$ in per cent. With a balanced budget the market clears where $100 + 20r = 160 - 10r$, at $r = 2$ per cent and investment of £140bn. Now let the government borrow £30bn. Demand for funds becomes $I + 30$, the market clears at $r = 3$ per cent, and private investment falls to £130bn. Of the £30bn borrowed, £10bn came from crowded-out investment and £20bn from extra saving.

$$\frac{\text{fall in investment}}{\text{government borrowing}} = \frac{10}{20 + 10} = \frac{1}{3}\quad(\#ecrowd)$$

The fraction crowded out is the responsiveness of investment to the interest rate divided by the combined responsiveness of saving and investment, the same algebra as the tax incidence of ({eq:e38}). Borrowing is "paid for" by whichever side of the funds market responds less.

![Figure {fig:lfunds}. The market for loanable funds. Government borrowing of £30bn raises the demand for funds, lifts the real interest rate from 2 to 3 per cent, and crowds out £10bn of private investment.](figs/lfunds.png)

Resource crowding out is more direct: if the economy is at capacity, the workers and materials the government buys are taken from private use, so real output cannot rise and the extra spending shows up in prices. Both objections weaken when there is spare capacity and idle labour, and when the central bank holds interest rates down, which is why the strength of the fiscal multiplier depends on the state of the economy rather than being a constant.

Whether a debt is sustainable depends on the interest rate, growth and the primary balance together. Let $b$ be debt as a share of GDP, $r$ the real interest rate on the debt, $g$ the growth rate of real GDP, and $\psi$ the primary surplus (taxes less spending other than interest) as a share of GDP. Each year interest adds $rb$ to the ratio, growth of the denominator removes roughly $gb$, and the primary surplus pays down $\psi$:

$$\Delta b \approx (r - g)\,b - \psi\quad(\#edebt)$$

The debt ratio is stable when $\psi = (r - g)b$. With debt at 100 per cent of GDP, close to the UK's level in the mid-2020s, a real interest rate of 2 per cent and growth of 1.5 per cent, stabilising the ratio needs a primary surplus of 0.5 per cent of GDP. If $r$ is below $g$, as it was for most of the 2010s, the ratio can fall even while the government runs a primary deficit; if $r$ rises above $g$, the same policy makes the ratio climb. That is why a rise in interest rates changes the fiscal arithmetic so sharply, and why markets' confidence in a government's plans feeds back into $r$ itself.

Set against monetary policy, fiscal policy has a different set of strengths, and which one is the right instrument depends on the situation rather than on principle:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
                      Monetary policy                      Fiscal policy
  ------------------- ------------------------------------ --------------------------------------------
  Instrument          Bank Rate, asset purchases           government spending and taxation

  Who decides         the MPC, independent of government   the Treasury, politically accountable

  Speed of decision   eight meetings a year, fast          tied to the Budget cycle, slow

  Speed of effect     18 to 24 months                      faster where projects are ready to run

  Targeting           economy-wide and blunt               can be aimed at regions, sectors or groups

  Main limit          the zero lower bound                 crowding out and debt sustainability
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Taxes also change the incentive to earn the income being taxed, which is the content of the Laffer curve. Revenue is zero at a zero rate and also at a 100 per cent rate, at which nobody declares taxable income, so it must peak somewhere between. Where it peaks depends on how strongly the tax base responds. Suppose the base shrinks as the share people keep, $1 - \tau$, falls, so that $Y(\tau) = Y_{0}(1 - \tau)^{\varepsilon}$, where $Y_{0}$ is the base with no tax and $\varepsilon$ is the elasticity of the base with respect to $1 - \tau$. Revenue is $T(\tau) = \tau Y_{0}(1 - \tau)^{\varepsilon}$, and

$$\frac{dT}{d\tau} = Y_{0}(1 - \tau)^{\varepsilon - 1}\left\lbrack (1 - \tau) - \varepsilon\tau \right\rbrack = 0\quad \Rightarrow \quad \tau^{*} = \frac{1}{1 + \varepsilon}\quad(\#elaffer)$$

The bracket is the product rule again: raising the rate gains revenue on the existing base and loses it on the base that shrinks. If the base barely responds, $\varepsilon = 0.25$, revenue peaks at a rate of 80 per cent; if it responds strongly, $\varepsilon = 2$, the peak is at 33 per cent. The curve's shape is not in dispute. Its peak is an empirical question about $\varepsilon$, which differs between taxes and between groups of taxpayers.

![Figure {fig:laffer}. The Laffer curve for two values of the elasticity of the tax base. Revenue peaks at $\tau^{*} = 1/(1 + \varepsilon)$, at 50 per cent when $\varepsilon = 1$ and at 33 per cent when $\varepsilon = 2$.](figs/laffer.png)

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The fiscal multiplier assumes spare capacity and a central bank that does not offset the stimulus with higher rates; with either missing, crowding out does most of the work.\
> The loanable funds example takes the saving and investment schedules as given; in a recession, the extra income generated by the multiplier also raises saving, so less investment is displaced.\
> The government is assumed able to borrow at a stable rate, which depends on markets continuing to believe the debt is sustainable.\
> Discretionary policy assumes the downturn is recognised, the measure passed and the money spent while it is still needed, and those lags often exceed the length of a recession.
