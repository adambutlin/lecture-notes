## Government intervention in markets {#s14}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Specific tax**: a tax of a fixed amount per unit sold, which moves supply up by exactly that amount.\
> **Ad valorem tax**: a tax set as a percentage of the price, such as VAT.\
> **Tax incidence**: how the burden divides between buyers and sellers, decided by relative elasticities rather than by who hands the money over.\
> **Subsidy**: a payment per unit to producers, which moves supply down by exactly that amount.\
> **Maximum price**: a legal ceiling, which creates excess demand if it binds.\
> **Minimum price**: a legal floor, which creates excess supply if it binds.

A specific tax of $t$ per unit changes nothing about what buyers want and everything about what sellers receive. Firms respond to the price they keep, $P - t$, so supply becomes

$$Q_{S} = \mu + \lambda(P - t)\quad(\#e36)$$

A seller handing £$t$ of every sale to the government decides how much to make on the basis of the amount left over. On the diagram this moves the supply curve **up** by exactly £$t$: to part with any given quantity, firms now need the old price plus the tax.

Two prices now have to be distinguished: $P_{D}$, the price on the demand curve that buyers pay at the till, and $P_{S} = P_{D} - t$, the price on the supply curve that sellers keep. Running the equilibrium calculation of ({eq:e24}) again with the taxed supply curve:

$$\alpha - \beta P_{D} + \gamma Y = \mu + \lambda(P_{D} - t)\quad \Rightarrow \quad P_{D} = \frac{\alpha - \mu + \gamma Y + \lambda t}{\beta + \lambda}\quad(\#e37)$$

Comparing ({eq:e37}) with ({eq:e24}), the only change is the extra $\lambda t$ on the top, so the price buyers pay has risen, but by less than the whole tax. Differentiating both prices with respect to the tax:

$$\frac{dP_{D}}{dt} = \frac{\lambda}{\beta + \lambda},\quad\quad\frac{dP_{S}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left( P_{D} - t \right) = \frac{\lambda}{\beta + \lambda} - 1 = - \frac{\beta}{\beta + \lambda}\quad(\#e38)$$

For each £1 of tax, the price buyers pay rises by $\lambda/(\beta + \lambda)$ of a pound and the price sellers keep falls by $\beta/(\beta + \lambda)$ of a pound. The two shares sum to one and both lie strictly between zero and one. The side of the market that responds **less** to price bears **more** of the tax, because it has fewer alternatives to escape into. Whether the government collects the money from the seller or the buyer never enters the algebra, which is why the legal point of collection does not determine who actually pays.

![Figure {fig:f20}. A specific tax drives a vertical wedge $t$ between the price buyers pay, $P_{D}$, and the price sellers keep, $P_{S}$. A subsidy drives the same wedge the other way, and both create a welfare loss.](figs/f20.png)

Tax revenue is $t \times Q_{t}$, the shaded rectangle. The grey triangle is the **welfare loss**: units that were worth more to buyers than they cost to make, and which now do not get made. Its size grows with the elasticities, because elastic curves mean the tax destroys more transactions.

Demand for petrol is inelastic, so $\beta$ is small, $\lambda/(\beta + \lambda)$ is close to one, and motorists bear nearly the whole duty. Quantity barely falls, so revenue is large and predictable, which is why governments like the tax. The same fact makes fuel duty a weak instrument if the goal is to cut consumption rather than raise money.

A subsidy of $s$ per unit is a tax of $- s$. It moves supply down by $s$, lowers the price buyers pay, raises the price sellers receive, splits its benefit between them by the same elasticity rule, and costs the government $s \times Q_{s}$. It also creates a welfare loss, unless it is correcting a positive externality: the extra units between $Q^{*}$ and $Q_{s}$ cost more to make than buyers value them at, and the triangle between the supply and demand curves over that range measures the waste. The cost to the government is larger than the combined gain to buyers and sellers by exactly that triangle.

A **price control** does not shift a curve; it forbids the market from reaching $P^{*}$. The size of the resulting disequilibrium follows from ({eq:e20}) and ({eq:e23}) directly. Since $Q_{D} = Q_{S}$ at $P^{*}$,

$$Q_{D} - Q_{S} = (\alpha - \mu + \gamma Y) - (\beta + \lambda)P = (\beta + \lambda)\left( P^{*} - P \right)\quad(\#e39)$$

The gap between what buyers want and what sellers offer at any price is the distance of that price from equilibrium multiplied by the combined responsiveness of the two sides. A maximum price below $P^{*}$ creates a shortage proportional to how far below it sits; a minimum price above $P^{*}$ creates a surplus by the same formula with the sign reversed. The minimum wage of section {sec:slab} is the labour-market case.

Either way, the quantity actually traded is the smaller of the two, since nobody can be made to buy or sell more than they want. Under a maximum price only $Q_{S}$ is sold. The buyers who get the good gain the price cut on every unit they buy, a transfer from sellers; the units between $Q_{S}$ and the equilibrium quantity $Q^{*}$ are not traded at all, and the triangle on them is the welfare loss.

![Figure {fig:f21}. Price controls. The quantity traded is the short side of the market. Under a maximum price, buyers who are served gain a transfer from sellers, and the triangle is the welfare loss on units no longer traded.](figs/f21.png)

A binding maximum price stops the price doing the rationing without stopping the excess demand, so something else rations instead: queues, waiting lists, allocation by landlord preference, or a black market in which the effective price exceeds $P^{*}$. Rent controls transfer income to whoever already holds a tenancy and reduce the incentive to supply new housing, so their long-run effect is usually worse than their short-run effect: supply is more elastic in the long run, so by ({eq:e39}) the shortage grows.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The tax and subsidy analysis assumes competitive markets with given demand and supply curves, and no externality; with an externality present, section {sec:s13} shows a tax or subsidy can raise welfare rather than lower it.\
> Under a maximum price, the welfare triangle assumes the scarce units go to the buyers who value them most. If they are allocated by queue or by chance, the loss is larger.
