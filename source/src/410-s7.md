## Demand {#s7}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Demand**: the quantity buyers are willing and able to buy at each price in a given period.\
> **Law of demand**: quantity demanded falls as price rises, other things equal.\
> **Ceteris paribus**: the assumption that everything not named in the model is held still.\
> **Willingness to pay**: the most a buyer would pay for a unit, measured in pounds.\
> **Movement along** a demand curve is caused by a change in the good's own price; a **shift** of it is caused by a change in anything else.

The household of section {sec:s1} has a demand curve, but not one that can be read as a measure of value. Its utility is a ranking, not a quantity of pounds; it spends half its budget on $x$, so any change in the price of $x$ changes its real income substantially; and it buys $x^{*} = B/2p_{x} > 0$ at every price, however high, so it never reaches a price at which it would stop. The area under that curve, which section {sec:s11} will use to measure what buyers gain from a market, is infinite.

Most goods are not like that. A household that spends a small share of its budget on a good can treat the money left over as a single amount, worth £1 per pound, and its utility as

$$U = v(x) + (B - p_{x}x)\quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{dU}{dx} = v'(x) - p_{x} = 0\quad \Rightarrow \quad v'(x) = p_{x}\quad(\#eql)$$

Here $v(x)$ is the value in pounds of consuming $x$ units and $B - p_{x}x$ is what is left to spend on everything else. The household keeps buying while the value of the next unit, $v'(x)$, is at least its price, and stops where the two are equal. Because $v'$ falls as $x$ rises, a lower price brings more units over that line. The demand curve is now the marginal-benefit curve, its height at any quantity is the willingness to pay for the last unit, and that is the reading every later diagram in these notes relies on. The step that justifies it is the small budget share: a change in price then barely changes real income, so the demand curve reflects value rather than the household's changing ability to pay.

Adding up all buyers at each price gives market demand, which we write as a function:

$$Q_{D} = \alpha - \beta P + \gamma Y\quad(\#e20)$$

$Q_{D}$ is the quantity demanded per period; $P$ is the good's own price; $Y$ is consumer income; $\alpha$ is the intercept, one number standing in for everything else that moves demand (tastes, population, the prices of related goods, expectations); $\beta > 0$ is how many units demand falls by for each £1 on the price; and $\gamma \neq 0$ is how many units it changes by for each extra £1 of income. Greek letters are used for the market demand and supply parameters throughout, so that they can never be confused with the firm's own $a$ and $b$ in section {sec:s5}, with cost symbols, or with the differentiation symbol $d$.

$$\frac{\partial Q_{D}}{\partial P} = - \beta < 0,\quad\quad\frac{\partial Q_{D}}{\partial Y} = \gamma\quad(\#e21)$$

With income held fixed, each £1 added to the price cuts the quantity demanded by $\beta$ units, which is the law of demand. With the price held fixed, each extra £1 of income changes the quantity demanded by $\gamma$ units, and the sign of $\gamma$ classifies the good: positive for a normal good, negative for an inferior one.

The distinction between the two derivatives is the distinction between a shift and a movement. A rise in $Y$ raises $Q_{D}$ **at every price**, because $P$ is held fixed while we differentiate, so the whole curve moves. A change in $P$ changes nothing inside the function, so it only moves the buyer along the curve already there.

Diagrams put $P$ on the vertical axis, so plot the inverse demand function obtained by rearranging ({eq:e20}):

$$P = \underset{\text{vertical intercept}}{\underbrace{\frac{\alpha + \gamma Y}{\beta}}} + \underset{\text{gradient}}{\underbrace{\left( - \frac{1}{\beta} \right)}}Q_{D}\quad(\#e22)$$

The height of the curve above any quantity is the willingness to pay of the buyer of that last unit. The vertical intercept $(\alpha + \gamma Y)/\beta$ is the price at which the very first unit would be bought, and the gradient $- 1/\beta$ says how fast willingness to pay falls as more units are sold. A rise in income raises the intercept and leaves the gradient alone, so the whole line moves **up** by $\gamma\Delta Y/\beta$, parallel to itself. Buyers will now pay that much more for each unit than they would before.

![Figure {fig:f9}. Movement along demand versus a shift of demand. In (a) only price changes. In (b) income rises, so at every quantity buyers will pay $\gamma\Delta Y/\beta$ more than before.](figs/f9.png)

For concert tickets, a fixed venue means a fixed number of seats. If an artist becomes suddenly famous, $\alpha$ rises, the curve moves up, and at the old face value far more people want tickets than there are seats. Nothing about the seats has changed; the willingness to pay has.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Buyers are assumed to respond to price on average and at a point in time, which is not the same as assuming that anybody is a calculating machine.\
> Everything inside $\alpha$ and $Y$ is held still, so the curve is a schedule of intentions at one moment rather than a forecast of what will happen.\
> Reading the curve as willingness to pay in pounds assumes the good takes a small share of buyers' budgets, as in ({eq:eql}). For housing or energy, where a price change alters real income substantially, it is an approximation.
