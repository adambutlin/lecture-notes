## Exchange rates and the balance of payments {#s25}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Exchange rate**: the price of one currency in terms of another.\
> **Appreciation** and **depreciation**: a rise and a fall in that price under a floating regime; **revaluation** and **devaluation** are the same changes made deliberately under a fixed regime.\
> **Marshall-Lerner condition**: a depreciation improves the trade balance only if the two demand elasticities sum to more than one.\
> **J-curve**: the path by which the balance worsens before it improves, because elasticities are low at first.\
> **Current account**: trade in goods and services plus primary and secondary income.\
> **Financial account**: transactions in assets, which must offset the current account.

An exchange rate is a price, so it is determined the way section {sec:s9} determines any price, in a market with a demand side and a supply side. Sterling is demanded by anyone who needs to pay in pounds: foreign buyers of UK exports, foreign investors buying UK assets, and speculators expecting appreciation. Sterling is supplied by anyone converting out of it: UK importers, UK investors buying foreign assets, and speculators going the other way.

![Figure {fig:f32}. The market for sterling. A rise in demand for pounds appreciates the currency.](figs/f32.png)

Higher UK interest rates relative to abroad attract capital inflows and appreciate the pound, which is the exchange-rate channel of section {sec:s22}. Higher UK inflation relative to trading partners makes UK goods less competitive and depreciates it. A stronger current account position, faster relative growth, and shifts in speculative sentiment all move it. Under a **floating** regime the rate is left to the market. Under a **fixed** regime the central bank must buy and sell reserves to hold a parity, and if capital moves freely across borders it loses control of domestic interest rates in doing so, since any gap between its rate and foreign rates would draw in or drive out capital until the parity broke.

A depreciation makes exports cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer in sterling, but whether it improves the trade balance depends on how much quantities respond. Write $e$ for the exchange rate in dollars per pound, $P_{X}$ for the sterling price of UK exports and $P_{M}^{*}$ for the dollar price of imports, both held fixed in their own currencies. Foreign buyers pay $eP_{X}$ for UK exports and UK buyers pay $P_{M}^{*}/e$ for imports, and the trade balance in pounds is

$$TB = P_{X}X - \frac{P_{M}^{*}}{e}M\quad(\#etb)$$

where $X$ and $M$ are the volumes of exports and imports. A 1 per cent fall in $e$ lowers the foreign price of exports by 1 per cent, so export volume rises by $|PED_{X}|$ per cent; it raises the sterling price of imports by 1 per cent, so import volume falls by $|PED_{M}|$ per cent, but every unit still imported costs 1 per cent more. Starting from balanced trade, with exports and imports each worth $V$, differentiating ({eq:etb}) and collecting the three effects gives

$$\frac{\Delta TB}{V} = \left( |PED_{X}| + |PED_{M}| - 1 \right) \times \left( - \frac{\Delta e}{e} \right)\quad(\#e51)$$

A depreciation, $\Delta e < 0$, improves the balance only if $|PED_{X}| + |PED_{M}| > 1$: that is the Marshall-Lerner condition. The minus one is the import bill rising on the units still bought; the two elasticities are the volume responses. With both elasticities at 0.6, a 10 per cent depreciation improves the balance by $(1.2 - 1) \times 10 = 2$ per cent of trade, even though each demand is inelastic. With both at 0.3, the same depreciation worsens it by 4 per cent. What matters is the sum, not whether each demand is elastic.

Elasticities are low immediately after a depreciation, because contracts are in place and buyers take time to switch, so the sum is often below one at first and above it later. The balance therefore typically deteriorates before it improves. That path is the J-curve, and it is an application of the point in section {sec:s10} that elasticity rises with time.

![Figure {fig:jcurve}. The J-curve. After a depreciation the trade balance first worsens, while elasticities are low, and then improves as volumes adjust.](figs/jcurve.png)

The **balance of payments** records all transactions with the rest of the world. The current account covers trade in goods and services, primary income (profits, interest and dividends earned abroad) and secondary income (transfers). The **capital and financial account** records transactions in assets. The two must sum to zero, apart from errors in measurement, because a country that buys more goods than it sells must be selling assets or borrowing to pay for the difference. This is another accounting identity, so a current account deficit is not in itself evidence of anything wrong; what matters is what the offsetting inflow is financing and whether it is likely to continue.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The market for sterling is treated like any other market, which assumes the exchange rate floats freely.\
> The Marshall-Lerner condition assumes export and import prices in their own currencies stay put when the exchange rate moves, so the whole change passes through to buyers; in practice firms absorb part of it in their margins. It also starts from balanced trade; with an initial deficit, the condition needed is slightly stricter.\
> The balance of payments must balance as an identity, but that says nothing about whether a given deficit can go on being financed.
