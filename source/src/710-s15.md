## Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost {#s15}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Scarcity**: the gap between unlimited wants and limited resources, which is what forces choice.\
> **Opportunity cost**: the value of the best alternative given up, met at firm level in section {sec:s6} and applied here to the whole economy.\
> **Production possibility frontier**: the boundary of the combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its current resources and technology.\
> **Economic growth**: an outward shift of that frontier. Points inside it are attainable but inefficient, and points outside it are unattainable this year.

Wants are effectively unlimited; the resources available in any period are not, and the gap between the two forces every choice to carry an opportunity cost.

Put the whole economy's choice in two goods, $x$ (consumer goods) and $y$ (capital goods). Resources and technology fix which combinations are feasible. The boundary of the feasible set is the production possibility frontier, written $y = f(x)$, and the opportunity cost of one more unit of $x$ is the gradient:

$$\text{opportunity cost of one more }x = - \frac{dy}{dx}\quad(\#e40)$$

The cost of one extra unit of consumer goods is the quantity of capital goods given up to release the resources for it, which is the steepness of the frontier at the current point; the minus sign makes the cost come out positive, since the frontier slopes downward.

If every resource were equally good at making both goods, the frontier would be a straight line and opportunity cost would be constant. Resources are not equally good at both, so transferring the workers and machines best suited to $y$ into $x$ costs progressively more $y$ per extra $x$. That rising sacrifice is what makes the frontier bow outwards.

![Figure {fig:f22}. The production possibility frontier. The gradient at any point is the opportunity cost of consumer goods, measured in capital goods.](figs/f22.png)

A point inside the frontier means resources are unemployed or badly allocated, so more of both goods is possible. A point outside is unattainable this year. Economic growth moves the frontier outwards, which is why the choice between $x$ and $y$ matters beyond this year: capital goods are the inputs that shift the frontier for next year, and consumer goods are not.

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> Fixed quantity and quality of resources.\
> Fixed technology.\
> Two goods.\
> All resources fully and efficiently used on the frontier itself.
