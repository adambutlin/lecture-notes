## Market failure {#s13}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Market failure**: an allocation in which the market quantity differs from the quantity that would maximise social surplus.\
> **Externality**: a cost or benefit falling on someone who is not party to the transaction.\
> **Marginal social cost** and **marginal social benefit**: the private figure plus the external one.\
> **Public good**: a good that is non-rival and non-excludable, so no market forms at all.\
> **Rival**: one person's consumption reduces what is left for others; **excludable**: people who do not pay can be prevented from consuming.\
> **Club good**: excludable but non-rival; **common good**: rival but non-excludable.\
> **Merit good**: one under-consumed because buyers underestimate its private benefit; a **demerit good** is the reverse.\
> **Asymmetric information**: one side of a transaction knowing more than the other.\
> **Government failure**: intervention that leaves the allocation worse than it found it.

Section {sec:s11} measured efficiency using **private** benefit and cost. Market failure is what happens when private and social values diverge, so that even a perfectly competitive market reaches the wrong quantity. Write the social magnitudes as the private ones plus whatever spills over onto third parties:

$$MSC = MPC + MEC,\quad\quad MSB = MPB + MEB\quad(\#e34)$$

Marginal social cost $MSC$ is the full cost to society of one more unit, **marginal private cost** $MPC$ is the part that lands on the producer, and **marginal external cost** $MEC$ is the spillover onto people with no part in the transaction. **Marginal external benefit** $MEB$ measures the same thing for benefits. When the external terms are zero the social and private curves coincide, which is the case section {sec:s11} quietly assumed.

The market equates $MPB$ and $MPC$; efficiency requires $MSB = MSC$. A factory whose production imposes an external cost, say emissions, has $MEC > 0$, so $MSC$ lies above $MPC$ and the efficient quantity $Q^{*}$ is below the market quantity $Q_{mkt}$. The value lost is the accumulated excess of social cost over social benefit on the units that should not have been produced, the construction of ({eq:e30}) run over the range where benefit and cost have swapped places:

$$\text{welfare loss} = \int_{Q^{*}}^{Q_{mkt}}\left\lbrack MSC(q) - MSB(q) \right\rbrack dq\quad(\#e35)$$

![Figure {fig:f19}. A negative production externality and a positive consumption externality. In both cases the market quantity $Q_{mkt}$ differs from the socially efficient quantity $Q^{*}$.](figs/f19.png)

A **Pigouvian tax** set at $t^{*} = MEC\left( Q^{*} \right)$ raises the firm's private marginal cost until it coincides with social marginal cost at the efficient quantity, so the profit-maximising choice and the efficient choice become the same. Carbon pricing applies the idea. Its practical difficulty is measurement: $MEC$ for a tonne of carbon is contested, and a tax set at the wrong level moves the quantity in the right direction by the wrong amount. Tradable permits fix the quantity instead and let the price emerge, which is the better instrument when hitting a quantity target matters more than the cost of doing so.

Education and vaccination generate benefits to people other than the buyer, so $MSB$ exceeds $MPB$, the market under-consumes, and a subsidy or direct provision closes the gap.

A good that is **non-rival** (my consumption does not reduce yours) and **non-excludable** (I cannot prevent you consuming it) cannot be sold, because everyone's best response is to wait for someone else to pay: the free-rider problem. National defence and flood barriers are not under-provided by the market but not provided at all; the market is **missing** rather than merely inefficient.

Rivalry and excludability are separate properties, and crossing them gives four kinds of good. Only the private good, rival and excludable, is one that a competitive market handles without help, because only there does the buyer both bear the full cost of consuming and get the full benefit. A **common good**, such as an ocean fish stock, is rival but non-excludable: every boat's catch leaves fewer fish for the others and nobody can be kept out, so each fisher ignores the cost their catch imposes on the rest and the stock is overused. That is the tragedy of the commons, a negative externality of the kind in Figure {fig:f19}(a), and the remedies are quotas, licences or ownership rights that make the resource excludable. A **club good**, such as a streaming service, is excludable but non-rival: the firm can charge for access, so the market supplies it, but an extra viewer costs nothing to serve, so any positive price turns away people whose viewing would have cost society nothing.

**Information failures** arise when one side knows more than the other. Adverse selection can shrink or destroy a market: insurers who cannot tell risky from safe customers must charge an average premium, which drives the safe customers out and raises the average again. Moral hazard is the related problem of behaviour changing once insured. **Merit goods** are under-consumed because buyers underestimate the private benefit, and **demerit goods** over-consumed for the same reason in reverse; section {sec:sbeh} supplies several of the reasons.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Failure                What goes wrong                      Market outcome                    Standard remedy
  ---------------------- ------------------------------------ --------------------------------- ------------------------------------------
  Negative externality   $MSC > MPC$                          over-production                   tax set at $MEC$, tradable permits

  Positive externality   $MSB > MPB$                          under-consumption                 subsidy, or direct provision

  Public good            non-rival and non-excludable         no market forms at all            provision funded from taxation

  Information failure    one side knows more than the other   adverse selection, moral hazard   disclosure rules, regulation, compulsion
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Identifying a market failure is a diagnosis, not by itself a case for intervention. Governments face the same information problem they are trying to fix, respond to political incentives, and create their own distortions, as section {sec:s14} shows for price controls. Government failure is the relevant comparison, and the right question is never "is the market perfect" but "which imperfect arrangement is better here".

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> External costs and benefits can be measured in pounds and added to private ones.\
> The remedies assume the government knows the size of the externality and can enforce the tax, subsidy or permit scheme at reasonable cost.
