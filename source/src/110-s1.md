## Consumer choice {#s1}

> **DEFINITIONS.**\
> **Utility**: the satisfaction a consumer gets from consuming goods, which matters only as a ranking and not as a quantity.\
> **Total utility**: the satisfaction obtained from the whole quantity consumed.\
> **Marginal utility**: the addition to total utility from consuming one more unit.\
> **Law of diminishing marginal utility**: beyond some point, each extra unit of a good adds less to utility than the unit before it.\
> **Budget constraint**: the combinations of goods a consumer can just afford at given prices and income.\
> **Consumer equilibrium**: the affordable combination that gives the highest utility.\
> **Equimarginal principle**: utility is highest when the last pound spent on each good buys the same amount of utility.\
> **Normal good**: one whose demand rises with income; an **inferior good** is one whose demand falls.\
> **Price elasticity of demand (PED)**: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.

A household chooses quantities of two goods, $x$ and $y$. Its utility is

$$U(x,y) = \sqrt{xy} = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}\quad \Rightarrow \quad MU_{x} = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} = \frac{1}{2}x^{- 1/2}y^{1/2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{y}{x}}\quad(\#e1)$$

$U$ is a number standing for how satisfied the household is with a bundle of $x$ units of one good and $y$ of the other. The marginal utility of $x$ is the extra satisfaction one more unit of $x$ brings. Because $U$ depends on two variables, it is found by differentiating with respect to $x$ while holding $y$ still, and the curly $\partial$ in place of $d$ is the signal that the other variable is being held fixed. The square root makes the satisfaction from another unit of $x$ depend on how much $x$ the household already has:

$$\frac{\partial MU_{x}}{\partial x} = - \frac{1}{4}x^{- 3/2}y^{1/2} < 0\quad(\#e2)$$

The second derivative is negative at every quantity, so each extra unit of $x$ adds less than the one before it. That is the law of diminishing marginal utility, obtained here from the shape of the function, and section {sec:s2} meets its counterpart on the production side.

![Figure {fig:f1}. Utility and marginal utility for one good, with the quantity of the other held fixed.](figs/f1.png)

What the household can afford is the other half of the problem. With prices $p_{x}$ and $p_{y}$ and a budget of $B$ to spend:

$$p_{x}x + p_{y}y = B\quad \Rightarrow \quad y = \frac{B}{p_{y}} - \frac{p_{x}}{p_{y}}x\quad(\#e3)$$

Making $y$ the subject turns the budget into a straight line. The vertical intercept $B/p_{y}$ is how much $y$ the household could have if it bought no $x$ at all, and the gradient $- p_{x}/p_{y}$ is the rate at which the market lets it swap $x$ for $y$: the opportunity cost of $x$, expressed in units of $y$.

The constraint states exactly what $y$ must be once $x$ has been chosen, so substituting it into the utility function removes $y$ altogether and leaves an ordinary maximisation in one variable. One further step makes the algebra easy. Utility numbers carry no meaning beyond the order in which they rank bundles, so replacing $U$ with any increasing function of $U$ describes the same household and produces the same choice. Maximising $\sqrt{xy}$ and maximising $xy$ are therefore the same problem, and the second is far easier to differentiate. Write $V = U^{2}$ and substitute ({eq:e3}) into it:

$$V(x) = xy = x\left( \frac{B}{p_{y}} - \frac{p_{x}}{p_{y}}x \right) = \frac{Bx - p_{x}x^{2}}{p_{y}}\quad(\#e4)$$

Every combination this function describes is affordable, because affordability was built in by the substitution.

$$\frac{dV}{dx} = \frac{B - 2p_{x}x}{p_{y}} = 0\quad \Rightarrow \quad x^{*} = \frac{B}{2p_{x}},\quad\quad\frac{d^{2}V}{dx^{2}} = - \frac{2p_{x}}{p_{y}} < 0\quad(\#e5)$$

The second derivative is negative everywhere, so the turning point is a maximum. Putting $x^{*}$ back into the budget line gives $y^{*} = B/2p_{y}$ by the same arithmetic, and the firm in section {sec:s6} will solve its problem in exactly this way.

![Figure {fig:f2}. The budget line with the chosen point on it, and the same problem after substitution, reduced to one variable with a single peak.](figs/f2.png)

Spending on $x$ is $p_{x}x^{*} = B/2$ whatever the price, so this household splits its budget evenly between the two goods. The even split comes from the square root; a different utility function would divide the budget differently. Differentiating the answer gives the demand curve:

$$\frac{dx^{*}}{dp_{x}} = - \frac{B}{2p_{x}^{2}} < 0,\quad\quad\frac{dx^{*}}{dB} = \frac{1}{2p_{x}} > 0\quad(\#e6)$$

The first derivative is negative at every price, which is the law of demand, here a conclusion rather than a starting point. The second is positive, so $x$ is a normal good. And because spending on $x$ stays at $B/2$ however the price moves, a 1 per cent rise in price must cut quantity by exactly 1 per cent: the price elasticity of demand is $- 1$ at every point on this curve, a property called unit elasticity that section {sec:s10} develops.

![Figure {fig:f3}. The demand curve traced out by the chosen quantity as the price varies. A and B are the two budget lines of the previous figure.](figs/f3.png)

The particular answer depends on the particular utility function, but the condition behind it does not. At the best affordable point the household cannot gain by moving a pound from one good to the other, so the last pound spent must buy the same utility in each:

$$\frac{MU_{x}}{p_{x}} = \frac{MU_{y}}{p_{y}}\quad(\#e7)$$

Each side is the utility bought by the last pound spent on that good. If the two differed, the household could move a pound from the good with the lower figure to the other and be better off, so it was not at the best point to begin with. Substituting the answers already found, where $y^{*}/x^{*} = p_{x}/p_{y}$, both sides collapse to the same expression:

$$\frac{MU_{x}}{p_{x}} = \frac{1}{2p_{x}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{x}}{p_{y}}} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{p_{x}p_{y}}} = \frac{1}{2p_{y}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{y}}{p_{x}}} = \frac{MU_{y}}{p_{y}}$$

> **ASSUMPTIONS.**\
> The household can rank any two bundles, and ranks them consistently: preferring A to B and B to C means preferring A to C.\
> More is preferred to less, which is why the budget is spent in full and the constraint holds as an equality.\
> Utility is ordinal. Only the ranking carries meaning, which is what allows $U$ to be replaced by $U^{2}$ without changing the answer.\
> Prices and the budget are given, the household is too small to move either, and there are only two goods, with everything else folded into $y$.\
> The function $\sqrt{xy}$ produces the even split of the budget. What survives any sensible choice of function is the downward-sloping demand curve and the equimarginal condition, not the halves.\
> Tastes are fixed and the household calculates correctly. Section {sec:sbeh} looks at what happens when it does not.
